# Primefree sequence

- [Русский](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D0%9F%D0%BE%D1%81%D0%BB%D0%B5%D0%B4%D0%BE%D0%B2%D0%B0%D1%82%D0%B5%D0%BB%D1%8C%D0%BD%D0%BE%D1%81%D1%82%D1%8C_%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%B7_%D0%BF%D1%80%D0%BE%D1%81%D1%82%D1%8B%D1%85_%D1%87%D0%B8%D1%81%D0%B5%D0%BB)
 [Edit links](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Special:EntityPage/Q7243388#sitelinks-wikipedia)                          From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia   Sequence of integer free of any prime number

In [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics), a **primefree sequence** is a [sequence of integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer_sequence) that does not contain any [prime numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number). More specifically, it usually means a [sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequence) defined by the same [recurrence relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recurrence_relation) as the [Fibonacci numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_number), but with different [initial conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_condition) causing all members of the sequence to be [composite numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composite_number) that do not all have a common [divisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisor). To put it algebraically, a sequence of this type is defined by an appropriate choice of two composite numbers *a*1 and *a*2, such that the [greatest common divisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greatest_common_divisor)      g c d  (  a  1   ,  a  2   )   {\displaystyle \mathrm {gcd} (a_{1},a_{2})}   is equal to 1, and such that for     n > 2   {\displaystyle n>2}   there are no primes in the sequence of numbers calculated from the formula

      a  n   =  a  n − 1   +  a  n − 2     {\displaystyle a_{n}=a_{n-1}+a_{n-2}}  .

The first primefree sequence of this type was published by [Ronald Graham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Graham) in 1964.

## Wilf's sequence

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Primefree_sequence&action=edit&section=1)]

A primefree sequence found by [Herbert Wilf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Wilf) has initial terms

      a  1   = 20615674205555510 ,  a  2   = 3794765361567513   {\displaystyle a_{1}=20615674205555510,a_{2}=3794765361567513}   (sequence [A083216](https://oeis.org/A083216) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences))

The [proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_proof) that every term of this sequence is composite relies on the periodicity of Fibonacci-like number sequences [modulo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_arithmetic) the members of a finite set of primes. For each prime     p   {\displaystyle p}  , the positions in the sequence where the numbers are divisible by     p   {\displaystyle p}   repeat in a periodic pattern, and different primes in the set have overlapping patterns that result in a [covering set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covering_set) for the whole sequence.

## Nontriviality

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Primefree_sequence&action=edit&section=2)]

The requirement that the initial terms of a primefree sequence be [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) is necessary for the question to be non-trivial. If the initial terms share a prime factor     p   {\displaystyle p}   (e.g., set      a  1   = x p   {\displaystyle a_{1}=xp}   and      a  2   = y p   {\displaystyle a_{2}=yp}   for some     x   {\displaystyle x}   and     y   {\displaystyle y}   both greater than 1), due to the [distributive property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributive_property) of [multiplication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplication)      a  3   = ( x + y ) p   {\displaystyle a_{3}=(x+y)p}   and more generally all subsequent values in the sequence will be multiples of     p   {\displaystyle p}  . In this case, all the numbers in the sequence will be composite, but for a trivial reason.

The order of the initial terms is also important. In [Paul Hoffman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Hoffman_(science_writer))'s biography of [Paul Erdős](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Erd%C5%91s), *[The man who loved only numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_man_who_loved_only_numbers)*, the Wilf sequence is cited but with the initial terms switched. The resulting sequence appears primefree for the first hundred terms or so, but term 138 is the 45-digit prime     439351292910452432574786963588089477522344721   {\displaystyle 439351292910452432574786963588089477522344721}  .[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primefree_sequence#cite_note-1)

Several other primefree sequences are known:

The sequence of this type with the smallest known initial terms has

      a  1   = 106276436867 ,  a  2   = 35256392432   {\displaystyle a_{1}=106276436867,a_{2}=35256392432}   (sequence [A221286](https://oeis.org/A221286) in the OEIS; Vsemirnov 2004).

In [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics), a **primefree sequence** is a [sequence of integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer_sequence) that does not contain any [prime numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number). More specifically, it usually means a [sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequence) defined by the same [recurrence relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recurrence_relation) as the [Fibonacci numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_number), but with different [initial conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_condition) causing all members of the sequence to be [composite numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composite_number) that do not all have a common [divisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisor). To put it algebraically, a sequence of this type is defined by an appropriate choice of two composite numbers *a*1 and *a*2, such that the [greatest common divisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greatest_common_divisor)      g c d  (  a  1   ,  a  2   )   {\displaystyle \mathrm {gcd} (a_{1},a_{2})}   is equal to 1, and such that for     n > 2   {\displaystyle n>2}   there are no primes in the sequence of numbers calculated from the formula

      a  n   =  a  n − 1   +  a  n − 2     {\displaystyle a_{n}=a_{n-1}+a_{n-2}}  .

The first primefree sequence of this type was published by [Ronald Graham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Graham) in 1964.

Wilf's sequence

# Wilf's sequence

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Primefree_sequence&action=edit&section=1)]

A primefree sequence found by [Herbert Wilf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Wilf) has initial terms

      a  1   = 20615674205555510 ,  a  2   = 3794765361567513   {\displaystyle a_{1}=20615674205555510,a_{2}=3794765361567513}   (sequence [A083216](https://oeis.org/A083216) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences))

The [proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_proof) that every term of this sequence is composite relies on the periodicity of Fibonacci-like number sequences [modulo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_arithmetic) the members of a finite set of primes. For each prime     p   {\displaystyle p}  , the positions in the sequence where the numbers are divisible by     p   {\displaystyle p}   repeat in a periodic pattern, and different primes in the set have overlapping patterns that result in a [covering set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covering_set) for the whole sequence.

Nontriviality

# Nontriviality

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Primefree_sequence&action=edit&section=2)]

The requirement that the initial terms of a primefree sequence be [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) is necessary for the question to be non-trivial. If the initial terms share a prime factor     p   {\displaystyle p}   (e.g., set      a  1   = x p   {\displaystyle a_{1}=xp}   and      a  2   = y p   {\displaystyle a_{2}=yp}   for some     x   {\displaystyle x}   and     y   {\displaystyle y}   both greater than 1), due to the [distributive property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributive_property) of [multiplication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplication)      a  3   = ( x + y ) p   {\displaystyle a_{3}=(x+y)p}   and more generally all subsequent values in the sequence will be multiples of     p   {\displaystyle p}  . In this case, all the numbers in the sequence will be composite, but for a trivial reason.

The order of the initial terms is also important. In [Paul Hoffman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Hoffman_(science_writer))'s biography of [Paul Erdős](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Erd%C5%91s), *[The man who loved only numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_man_who_loved_only_numbers)*, the Wilf sequence is cited but with the initial terms switched. The resulting sequence appears primefree for the first hundred terms or so, but term 138 is the 45-digit prime     439351292910452432574786963588089477522344721   {\displaystyle 439351292910452432574786963588089477522344721}  .[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primefree_sequence#cite_note-1)
