In [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics), a **Mersenne prime** is a [prime number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number) that is one less than a [power of two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_of_two). That is, it is a prime number [of the form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Of_the_form) *Mn* = 2*n* − 1 for some [integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer) *n*. They are named after [Marin Mersenne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marin_Mersenne), a French [Minim friar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minims_(religious_order)), who studied them in the early 17th century. If *n* is a [composite number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composite_number) then so is 2*n* − 1. Therefore, an equivalent definition of the Mersenne primes is that they are the prime numbers of the form *Mp* = 2*p* − 1 for some prime *p*.

The [exponents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponentiation) *n* that give Mersenne primes are 2, 3, 5, 7, 13, 17, 19, 31, ... (sequence [A000043](https://oeis.org/A000043) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences)) and the resulting Mersenne primes are [3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3_(number)), [7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/7_(number)), [31](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/31_(number)), [127](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/127_(number)), [8191](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/8191_(number)), 131071, 524287, [2147483647](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2,147,483,647), ... (sequence [A000668](https://oeis.org/A000668) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences)).

Numbers of the form *Mn* = 2*n* − 1 without the primality requirement may be called **Mersenne numbers**. Sometimes, however, Mersenne numbers are defined to have the additional requirement that *n* should be prime. The smallest composite Mersenne number with prime exponent *n* is 211 − 1 = 2047 = 23 × 89.

Mersenne primes were studied in antiquity because of their close [connection to perfect numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#Perfect_numbers): the [Euclid–Euler theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid%E2%80%93Euler_theorem) asserts a one-to-one correspondence between even perfect numbers and Mersenne primes. Many of the largest known primes are Mersenne primes because Mersenne numbers are easier to check for primality.

As of 2025[[ref]](http://primes.utm.edu/top20/page.php?id=4), [52 Mersenne primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Mersenne_primes_and_perfect_numbers) are known. The [largest known prime number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Largest_known_prime_number), 2136279841 − 1, is a Mersenne prime.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-GIMPS-2024-1)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-GIMPS-2018-2) Since 1997, all newly found Mersenne primes have been discovered by the [Great Internet Mersenne Prime Search](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Internet_Mersenne_Prime_Search), a [distributed computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributed_computing) project. In December 2020, a major milestone in the project was passed after all exponents below 100 million were checked at least once.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-3)

## About Mersenne primes

A basic [theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theorem) about Mersenne numbers states that if Mp is prime, then the exponent *p* must also be prime. This follows from the identity

          2  a b   − 1    = (  2  a   − 1 ) ⋅  (  1 +  2  a   +  2  2 a   +  2  3 a   + ⋯ +  2  ( b − 1 ) a    )        = (  2  b   − 1 ) ⋅  (  1 +  2  b   +  2  2 b   +  2  3 b   + ⋯ +  2  ( a − 1 ) b    )  .       {\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}2^{ab}-1&=(2^{a}-1)\cdot \left(1+2^{a}+2^{2a}+2^{3a}+\cdots +2^{(b-1)a}\right)\\&=(2^{b}-1)\cdot \left(1+2^{b}+2^{2b}+2^{3b}+\cdots +2^{(a-1)b}\right).\end{aligned}}}

This rules out primality for Mersenne numbers with a composite exponent, such as *M*4 = 24 − 1 = 15 = 3 × 5 = (22 − 1) × (1 + 22).

Though the above examples might suggest that Mp is prime for all primes p, this is not the case, and the smallest counterexample is the Mersenne number

 *M*11 = 211 − 1 = 2047 = 23 × 89.  Unsolved problem in mathematics Are there infinitely many Mersenne primes? [More unsolved problems in mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_unsolved_problems_in_mathematics)

Many fundamental questions about Mersenne primes remain unresolved. It is not even known whether the set of Mersenne primes is finite or infinite.

The [Lenstra–Pomerance–Wagstaff conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenstra%E2%80%93Pomerance%E2%80%93Wagstaff_conjecture) claims that there are infinitely many Mersenne primes and predicts their [order of growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_analysis) and frequency: For every number n, there should on average be about      e  γ   ⋅  log  2   ⁡ ( 10 ) ≈ 5.92   {\displaystyle e^{\gamma }\cdot \log _{2}(10)\approx 5.92}   primes p with n decimal digits for which *M**p* is prime. Here, γ is the [Euler–Mascheroni constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler%E2%80%93Mascheroni_constant).

It is also not known whether infinitely many Mersenne numbers with prime exponents are composite, although this would follow from widely believed [conjectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjecture) about prime numbers; for example, from the infinitude of [Sophie Germain primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophie_Germain_prime) [congruent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congruence_relation) to 3 ([mod 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_arithmetic)). For these primes p, 2*p* + 1 (which is also prime) will divide Mp, for example, 23 | *M*11, 47 | *M*23, 167 | *M*83, 263 | *M*131, 359 | *M*179, 383 | *M*191, 479 | *M*239, and 503 | *M*251 (sequence [A002515](https://oeis.org/A002515) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences)). For these primes p, 2*p* + 1 is congruent to 7 mod 8, so 2 is a [quadratic residue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_residue) mod 2*p* + 1, and the [multiplicative order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_order) of 2 mod 2*p* + 1 must divide        ( 2 p + 1 ) − 1  2   = p   {\textstyle {\frac {(2p+1)-1}{2}}=p}  . Since p is a prime, it must be p or 1. However, it cannot be 1 since Φ1(2) = 1 and 1 has no [prime factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factor), so it must be p. Hence, 2*p* + 1 divides Φ*p*(2) = 2*p* − 1 and 2*p* − 1 = *M**p* cannot be prime. The first four Mersenne primes are *M*2 = 3, *M*3 = 7, *M*5 = 31 and *M*7 = 127 and because the first Mersenne prime starts at *M*2, all Mersenne primes are congruent to 3 (mod 4). Other than *M*0 = 0 and *M*1 = 1, all other Mersenne numbers are also congruent to 3 (mod 4). Consequently, in the [prime factorization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factorization) of a Mersenne number ( ≥ *M*2) there must be at least one prime factor congruent to 3 (mod 4).

The evidence at hand suggests that a randomly selected Mersenne number is much more likely to be prime than an arbitrary randomly selected odd integer of similar size.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-Wagstaff-4) Nonetheless, prime values of Mp appear to grow increasingly sparse as p increases. For example, eight of the first 11 primes p give rise to a Mersenne prime Mp (the correct terms on Mersenne's original list), while Mp is prime for only 43 of the first two million prime numbers (up to 32452843).

Since Mersenne numbers grow very rapidly, the search for Mersenne primes is a difficult task, even though there is a simple efficient test to determine whether a given Mersenne number is prime: the [Lucas–Lehmer primality test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucas%E2%80%93Lehmer_primality_test) (LLT), which makes it much easier to test the primality of Mersenne numbers than that of most other numbers of the same size. The search for the largest known prime has somewhat of a [cult following](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cult_following).[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)*] Consequently, a large amount of computer power has been expended searching for new Mersenne primes, much of which is now done using [distributed computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributed_computing).

Arithmetic modulo a Mersenne number is particularly efficient on a [binary computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_number), making them popular choices when a prime modulus is desired, such as the [Park–Miller random number generator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Park%E2%80%93Miller_random_number_generator). To find a [primitive polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_polynomial_(field_theory)) of Mersenne number order requires knowing the factorization of that number, so Mersenne primes allow one to find primitive polynomials of very high order. Such [primitive trinomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_polynomial_(field_theory)#Primitive_trinomials) are used in [pseudorandom number generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudorandom_number_generator) with very large periods such as the [Mersenne twister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_twister), generalized shift register and [Lagged Fibonacci generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagged_Fibonacci_generator).

## Perfect numbers

 Main article: [Euclid–Euler theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid%E2%80%93Euler_theorem)

Mersenne primes *M**p* are closely connected to [perfect numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_number). In the 4th century BC, [Euclid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid) proved that if 2*p* − 1 is prime, then 2*p* − 1(2*p* − 1) is a perfect number. In the 18th century, [Leonhard Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonhard_Euler) proved that, conversely, all even perfect numbers have this form.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-5) This is known as the [Euclid–Euler theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid%E2%80%93Euler_theorem). It is unknown whether there are any [odd perfect numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_perfect_number).

## History

Mersenne primes take their name from the 17th-century [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_people) scholar [Marin Mersenne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marin_Mersenne), who compiled what was supposed to be a list of Mersenne primes with exponents up to 257. The exponents listed by Mersenne in 1644 were as follows:

 2, 3, 5, 7, 13, 17, 19, 31, 67, 127, 257.

His list replicated the known primes of his time with exponents up to 19. His next entry, 31, was correct, but the list then became largely incorrect, as Mersenne mistakenly included *M*67 and *M*257 (which are composite) and omitted *M*61, *M*89, and *M*107 (which are prime). Mersenne gave little indication of how he came up with his list.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-6)

[Édouard Lucas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89douard_Lucas) proved in 1876 that *M*127 is indeed prime, as Mersenne claimed. This was the largest known prime number for 75 years until 1951, when Aimé Ferrier found a larger prime, (2148 + 1)/17, using a desk calculating machine.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-7): page 22  *M*61 was determined to be prime in 1883 by [Ivan Mikheevich Pervushin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Mikheevich_Pervushin), though Mersenne claimed it was composite, and for this reason it is sometimes called Pervushin's number.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)*] This was the second-largest known prime number, and it remained so until 1911. Lucas had shown another error in Mersenne's list in 1876 by demonstrating that *M*67 was composite without finding a factor. No factor was found until a famous talk by [Frank Nelson Cole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Nelson_Cole) in 1903.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-8) Without speaking a word, he went to a blackboard and raised 2 to the 67th power, then subtracted one, resulting in the number 147573952589676412927. On the other side of the board, he multiplied 193707721 × 761838257287 and got the same number, then returned to his seat (to applause) without speaking.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-9) He later said that the result had taken him "three years of Sundays" to find.[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-10) A correct list of all Mersenne primes in this number range was completed and rigorously verified only about three centuries after Mersenne published his list.

## Searching for Mersenne primes

Fast algorithms for finding Mersenne primes are available, and as of October 2024[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Mersenne_prime&action=edit), the seven [largest known prime numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Largest_known_prime_number) are Mersenne primes.

The first four Mersenne primes *M*2 = 3, *M*3 = 7, *M*5 = 31 and *M*7 = 127 were known in antiquity. The fifth, *M*13 = 8191, was discovered anonymously before 1461; the next two (*M*17 and *M*19) were found by [Pietro Cataldi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pietro_Cataldi) in 1588. After nearly two centuries, *M*31 was verified to be prime by [Leonhard Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonhard_Euler) in 1772. The next (in historical, not numerical order) was *M*127, found by [Édouard Lucas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89douard_Lucas) in 1876, then *M*61 by [Ivan Mikheevich Pervushin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Mikheevich_Pervushin) in 1883. Two more (*M*89 and *M*107) were found early in the 20th century, by [R. E. Powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._E._Powers) in 1911 and 1914, respectively.[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-11)

The most efficient method presently known for testing the primality of Mersenne numbers is the [Lucas–Lehmer primality test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucas%E2%80%93Lehmer_primality_test). Specifically, it can be shown that for prime *p* > 2, *Mp* = 2*p* − 1 is prime [if and only if](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/If_and_only_if) *Mp* divides *S**p* − 2, where *S*0 = 4 and *Sk* = (*S**k* − 1)2 − 2 for *k* > 0.

During the era of manual calculation, all previously untested exponents up to and including 257 were tested with the Lucas–Lehmer test and found to be composite. A notable contribution was made by retired Yale physics professor Horace Scudder Uhler, who did the calculations for exponents 157, 167, 193, 199, 227, and 229.[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-12) Unfortunately for those investigators, the interval they were testing contains the largest known relative gap between Mersenne primes: the next Mersenne prime exponent, 521, would turn out to be more than four times as large as the previous record of 127.

The vertical scale is logarithmic in the number of digits, thus being a log(log(*y*)) function in the value of the prime.

The search for Mersenne primes was revolutionized by the introduction of the electronic digital computer. [Alan Turing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Turing) searched for them on the [Manchester Mark 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Mark_1) in 1949,[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-13) but the first successful identification of a Mersenne prime, *M*521, by this means was achieved at 10:00 pm on January 30, 1952, using the U.S. [National Bureau of Standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bureau_of_Standards) [Western Automatic Computer (SWAC)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SWAC_(computer)) at the Institute for Numerical Analysis at the [University of California, Los Angeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Los_Angeles) (UCLA), under the direction of [D. H. Lehmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derrick_Henry_Lehmer), with a computer search program written and run by Prof. [R. M. Robinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raphael_M._Robinson). It was the first Mersenne prime to be identified in thirty-eight years; the next one, *M*607, was found by the computer a little less than two hours later. Three more – *M*1279, *M*2203, and *M*2281 – were found by the same program in the next several months. *M*4423 was the first prime discovered with more than 1000 digits, *M*44497 was the first with more than 10000, and *M*6972593 was the first with more than a million. In general, the number of digits in the decimal representation of *Mn* equals ⌊*n* log102⌋ + 1, where ⌊*x*⌋ denotes the [floor function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floor_function) (or equivalently ⌊log10*Mn*⌋ + 1).

In September 2008, mathematicians at UCLA participating in the [Great Internet Mersenne Prime Search](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Internet_Mersenne_Prime_Search) (GIMPS) won part of a $100000 prize from the [Electronic Frontier Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Frontier_Foundation) for their discovery of a very nearly 13-million-digit Mersenne prime. The prize, finally confirmed in October 2009, is for the first known prime with at least 10 million digits. The prime was found on a [Dell OptiPlex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dell_OptiPlex) 745 on August 23, 2008. This was the eighth Mersenne prime discovered at UCLA.[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-14)

On April 12, 2009, a GIMPS server log reported that a 47th Mersenne prime had possibly been found. The find was first noticed on June 4, 2009, and verified a week later. The prime is 242643801 − 1. Although it is chronologically the 47th Mersenne prime to be discovered, it is smaller than the largest known at the time, which was the 45th to be discovered.

On January 25, 2013, [Curtis Cooper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtis_Cooper_(mathematician)), a mathematician at the [University of Central Missouri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Central_Missouri), discovered a 48th Mersenne prime, 257885161 − 1 (a number with 17425170 digits), as a result of a search executed by a GIMPS server network.[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-15)

On January 19, 2016, Cooper published his discovery of a 49th Mersenne prime, 274207281 − 1 (a number with 22338618 digits), as a result of a search executed by a GIMPS server network.[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-GIMPS-2016-16)[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-17)[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-NYT-20160121-18) This was the fourth Mersenne prime discovered by Cooper and his team in the past ten years.

On September 2, 2016, the Great Internet Mersenne Prime Search finished verifying all tests below *M*37156667, thus officially confirming its position as the 45th Mersenne prime.[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-19)

On January 3, 2018, it was announced that Jonathan Pace, a 51-year-old electrical engineer living in [Germantown, Tennessee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germantown,_Tennessee), had found a 50th Mersenne prime, 277232917 − 1 (a number with 23249425 digits), as a result of a search executed by a GIMPS server network.[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-20) The discovery was made by a computer in the offices of a church in the same town.[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-21)[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-22)

On December 21, 2018, it was announced that The Great Internet Mersenne Prime Search (GIMPS) discovered a new prime number, 282589933 − 1, having 24862048 digits. A computer volunteered by Patrick Laroche from [Ocala, Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocala,_Florida) made the find on December 7, 2018.[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-23)

In late 2020, GIMPS began using a new technique to rule out potential Mersenne primes called the [Probable prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probable_prime) (PRP) test, based on development from Robert Gerbicz in 2017, and a simple way to verify tests developed by Krzysztof Pietrzak in 2018. Due to the low error rate and ease of proof, this nearly halved the computing time to rule out potential primes over the Lucas-Lehmer test (as two users would no longer have to perform the same test to confirm the other's result), although exponents passing the PRP test still require at least two Lucas-Lehmer tests to confirm their primality.[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-24)

On October 12, 2024, a user named Luke Durant from San Jose, California, found the current largest known Mersenne prime, 2136279841 − 1, having 41024320 digits. This marks the first Mersenne prime with an exponent over 100000000. This was announced on October 21, 2024.[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-25)

## Theorems about Mersenne numbers

Mersenne numbers are 0, 1, 3, 7, 15, 31, 63, ... (sequence [A000225](https://oeis.org/A000225) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences)).

1. If *a* and p are natural numbers such that *ap* − 1 is prime, then *a* = 2 or *p* = 1. **Proof**: *a* ≡ 1 ([mod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_arithmetic) *a* − 1). Then *ap* ≡ 1 (mod *a* − 1), so *ap* − 1 ≡ 0 (mod *a* − 1). Thus *a* − 1 | *ap* − 1. However, *ap* − 1 is prime, so *a* − 1 = *ap* − 1 or *a* − 1 = ±1. In the former case, *a* = *ap*, hence *a* = 0, 1 (which is a contradiction, as neither −1 nor 0 is prime) or *p* = 1. In the latter case, *a* = 2 or *a* = 0. If *a* = 0, however, 0*p* − 1 = 0 − 1 = −1, which is not prime. Therefore, *a* = 2.
2. If 2*p* − 1 is prime, then p is prime. **Proof**: Suppose that p is composite, hence can be written *p* = *ab* with a and *b* > 1. Then 2*p* − 1 = 2*ab* − 1 = (2*a*)*b* − 1 = (2*a* − 1)((2*a*)*b*−1 + (2*a*)*b*−2 + ... + 2*a* + 1) so 2*p* − 1 is composite. By contraposition, if 2*p* − 1 is prime then *p* is prime.
3. If p is an odd prime, then every prime q that divides 2*p* − 1 must be 1 plus a multiple of 2*p*. This holds even when 2*p* − 1 is prime. For example, 25 − 1 = 31 is prime, and 31 = 1 + 3 × (2 × 5). A composite example is 211 − 1 = 23 × 89, where 23 = 1 + (2 × 11) and 89 = 1 + 4 × (2 × 11). **Proof**: By [Fermat's little theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat's_little_theorem), q is a factor of 2*q*−1 − 1. Since q is a factor of 2*p* − 1, for all positive integers *c*, q is also a factor of 2*pc* − 1. Since p is prime and q is not a factor of 21 − 1, p is also the smallest positive integer x such that q is a factor of 2*x* − 1. As a result, for all positive integers x, q is a factor of 2*x* − 1 if and only if p is a factor of x. Therefore, since q is a factor of 2*q*−1 − 1, p is a factor of *q* − 1 so *q* ≡ 1 (mod *p*). Furthermore, since q is a factor of 2*p* − 1, which is odd, q is odd. Therefore, *q* ≡ 1 (mod 2*p*). This fact leads to a proof of [Euclid's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid's_theorem), which asserts the infinitude of primes, distinct from the proof written by Euclid: for every odd prime p, all primes dividing 2*p* − 1 are larger than p; thus there are always larger primes than any particular prime. It follows from this fact that for every prime *p* > 2, there is at least one prime of the form 2*kp* + 1 less than or equal to Mp, for some integer k.
4. If p is an odd prime, then every prime q that divides 2*p* − 1 is congruent to ±1 (mod 8). **Proof**: 2*p*+1 ≡ 2 (mod *q*), so 2⁠1/2⁠(p+1) is a square root of 2 mod *q*. By [quadratic reciprocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_reciprocity), every prime modulus in which the number 2 has a square root is congruent to ±1 (mod 8).
5. A Mersenne prime cannot be a [Wieferich prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wieferich_prime). **Proof**: We show if *p* = 2*m* − 1 is a Mersenne prime, then the congruence 2*p*−1 ≡ 1 (mod *p*2) does not hold. By Fermat's little theorem, *m* | *p* − 1. Therefore, one can write *p* − 1 = *mλ*. If the given congruence is satisfied, then *p*2 | 2*mλ* − 1, therefore 0 ≡ ⁠2*mλ* − 1/2*m* − 1⁠ = 1 + 2*m* + 22*m* + ... + 2(*λ* − 1)*m* ≡ *λ* mod (2*m* − 1). Hence p | *λ*, and therefore −1 = 0 (mod p), which is impossible.
6. If m and n are natural numbers then m and n are [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) if and only if 2*m* − 1 and 2*n* − 1 are coprime. Consequently, a prime number divides at most one prime-exponent Mersenne number.[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-26) That is, the set of [pernicious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pernicious_number) Mersenne numbers is pairwise coprime.
7. If p and 2*p* + 1 are both prime (meaning that p is a [Sophie Germain prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophie_Germain_prime)), and p is [congruent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congruence_relation) to 3 (mod 4), then 2*p* + 1 divides 2*p* − 1.[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-27) **Example**: 11 and 23 are both prime, and 11 = 2 × 4 + 3, so 23 divides 211 − 1. **Proof**: Let q be 2*p* + 1. By Fermat's little theorem, 22*p* ≡ 1 (mod *q*), so either 2*p* ≡ 1 (mod *q*) or 2*p* ≡ −1 (mod *q*). Supposing latter true, then 2*p*+1 = (2⁠1/2⁠(*p* + 1))2 ≡ −2 (mod *q*), so −2 would be a quadratic residue mod q. However, since p is congruent to 3 (mod 4), q is congruent to 7 (mod 8) and therefore 2 is a quadratic residue mod q. Also since q is congruent to 3 (mod 4), −1 is a quadratic nonresidue mod q, so −2 is the product of a residue and a nonresidue and hence it is a nonresidue, which is a contradiction. Hence, the former congruence must be true and 2*p* + 1 divides Mp.
8. All composite divisors of prime-exponent Mersenne numbers are [strong pseudoprimes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_pseudoprime) to the base 2.
9. With the exception of 1, a Mersenne number cannot be a perfect power. That is, and in accordance with [Mihăilescu's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalan's_conjecture), the equation 2*m* − 1 = *nk* has no solutions where m, n, and k are integers with *m* > 1 and *k* > 1.
10. The Mersenne number sequence is a member of the family of [Lucas sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucas_sequence). It is U*n*(3, 2). That is, Mersenne number *m**n* = 3*m**n*−1 − 2*m**n*−2 with *m*0 = 0 and *m*1 = 1.

## Factorization of composite Mersenne numbers

Since they are prime numbers, Mersenne primes are divisible only by 1 and themselves. However, not all Mersenne numbers are Mersenne primes. Mersenne numbers are very good test cases for the [special number field sieve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_number_field_sieve) algorithm, so often the largest number factorized with this algorithm has been a Mersenne number. As of June 2019[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Mersenne_prime&action=edit), 21193 − 1 is the record-holder,[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-28) having been factored with a variant of the special number field sieve that allows the factorization of several numbers at once. See *[Integer factorization records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer_factorization_records)* for links to more information. The special number field sieve can factorize numbers with more than one large factor. ## Mersenne numbers in nature and elsewhere

In the mathematical problem [Tower of Hanoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_of_Hanoi), optimally solving a puzzle with an *n*-disc tower requires *Mn* steps.[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-31) The number of rice grains on the whole chessboard in the [wheat and chessboard problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheat_and_chessboard_problem) is *M*64.[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-32)

The [asteroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid) with [minor planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_planet) number 8191 is named [8191 Mersenne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/8191_Mersenne) after Marin Mersenne, because 8191 is a Mersenne prime.[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-33)

In [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry), an integer [right triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_triangle) that is [primitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_triple) and has its even leg a power of 2 ( ≥ 4) generates a unique right triangle such that its [inradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inradius) is always a Mersenne number. For example, if the even leg is 2*n* + 1 then because it is primitive it constrains the odd leg to be 4*n* − 1, the [hypotenuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypotenuse) to be 4*n* + 1 and its inradius to be 2*n* − 1.[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-34)

Also in geometry, the number of [polytopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytopes) that are part of the family of polytopes formed by a [truncation operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_polytope#Operations) of a base regular polytope and its [dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_polyhedron#Dual_polytopes_and_tessellations) (excluding alternation) is equal to *Mn* where n is the dimension of the base polytope. For example, a [tesseract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesseract) and its dual the [hexadecachoron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexadecachoron) have *M4* = 15 different polytopes in its family formed by truncation operations.[[35]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-35)

## Mersenne–Fermat primes

A **Mersenne–Fermat number** is defined as ⁠2*pr* − 1/2*p**r* − 1 − 1⁠ with *p* prime, *r* natural number, and can be written as MF(*p*, *r*). When *r* = 1, it is a Mersenne number. When *p* = 2, it is a [Fermat number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat_number). The only known Mersenne–Fermat primes with *r* > 1 are

 MF(2, 2), MF(2, 3), MF(2, 4), MF(2, 5), MF(3, 2), MF(3, 3), MF(7, 2), and MF(59, 2).[[36]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-36)

In fact, MF(*p*, *r*) = *Φpr*(2), where *Φ* is the [cyclotomic polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclotomic_polynomial).

## Generalizations

 Main article: [Generalized Mersenne prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_Mersenne_prime)

The simplest generalized Mersenne primes are prime numbers of the form *f*(2*n*), where *f*(*x*) is a low-degree [polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial) with small integer [coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coefficients).[[37]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-37) An example is 264 − 232 + 1, in this case, *n* = 32, and *f*(*x*) = *x*2 − *x* + 1; another example is 2192 − 264 − 1, in this case, *n* = 64, and *f*(*x*) = *x*3 − *x* − 1.

It is also natural to try to generalize primes of the form 2*n* − 1 to primes of the form *b**n* − 1 (for *b* ≠ 2 and *n* > 1). However (see also *[§ Theorems about Mersenne numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#Theorems_about_Mersenne_numbers)* above), *b**n* − 1 is always divisible by *b* − 1, so unless the latter is a [unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_(ring_theory)), the former is not a prime. This can be remedied by allowing *b* to be an algebraic integer instead of an integer:

### Complex numbers

In the [ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_(mathematics)) of integers (on [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_number)), if *b* − 1 is a [unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_(ring_theory)), then *b* is either 2 or 0. But 2*n* − 1 are the usual Mersenne primes, and the formula 0*n* − 1 does not lead to anything interesting (since it is always −1 for all *n* > 0). Thus, we can regard a ring of "integers" on [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_number) instead of [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_number), like [Gaussian integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_integer) and [Eisenstein integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eisenstein_integer).

#### Gaussian Mersenne primes

If we regard the ring of [Gaussian integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_integer), we get the case *b* = 1 + *i* and *b* = 1 − *i*, and can ask ([WLOG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Without_loss_of_generality)) for which *n* the number (1 + *i*)*n* − 1 is a [Gaussian prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_prime), which will then be called a **Gaussian Mersenne prime**.[[38]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-38)

(1 + *i*)*n* − 1 is a Gaussian prime for the following *n*:

 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 19, 29, 47, 73, 79, 113, 151, 157, 163, 167, 239, 241, 283, 353, 367, 379, 457, 997, 1367, 3041, 10141, 14699, 27529, 49207, 77291, 85237, 106693, 160423, 203789, 364289, 991961, 1203793, 1667321, 3704053, 4792057, ... (sequence [A057429](https://oeis.org/A057429) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences))

Like the sequence of exponents for usual Mersenne primes, this sequence contains only (rational) prime numbers.

As for all Gaussian primes, the [norms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_integer#Norm_of_a_Gaussian_integer) (that is, squares of absolute values) of these numbers are rational primes:

 5, 13, 41, 113, 2113, 525313, 536903681, 140737471578113, ... (sequence [A182300](https://oeis.org/A182300) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences)).

#### Eisenstein Mersenne primes

One may encounter cases where such a Mersenne prime is also an *Eisenstein prime*, being of the form *b* = 1 + *ω* and *b* = 1 − *ω*. In these cases, such numbers are called **Eisenstein Mersenne primes**.

(1 + *ω*)*n* − 1 is an Eisenstein prime for the following *n*:

 2, 5, 7, 11, 17, 19, 79, 163, 193, 239, 317, 353, 659, 709, 1049, 1103, 1759, 2029, 5153, 7541, 9049, 10453, 23743, 255361, 534827, 2237561, ... (sequence [A066408](https://oeis.org/A066408) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences))

The norms (that is, squares of absolute values) of these Eisenstein primes are rational primes:

 7, 271, 2269, 176419, 129159847, 1162320517, ... (sequence [A066413](https://oeis.org/A066413) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences))

### Divide an integer

#### Repunit primes

 Main article: [Repunit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repunit)

The other way to deal with the fact that *b**n* − 1 is always divisible by *b* − 1, it is to simply take out this factor and ask which values of *n* make

         b  n   − 1   b − 1      {\displaystyle {\frac {b^{n}-1}{b-1}}}

be prime. (The integer *b* can be either positive or negative.) If, for example, we take *b* = 10, we get *n* values of:

 2, 19, 23, 317, 1031, 49081, 86453, 109297, 270343, ... (sequence [A004023](https://oeis.org/A004023) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences)),
corresponding to primes 11, 1111111111111111111, 11111111111111111111111, ... (sequence [A004022](https://oeis.org/A004022) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences)).

These primes are called repunit primes. Another example is when we take *b* = −12, we get *n* values of:

#### Other generalized Mersenne primes

Another generalized Mersenne number is

         a  n   −  b  n     a − b      {\displaystyle {\frac {a^{n}-b^{n}}{a-b}}}

with a, b any [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) integers, *a* > 1 and −*a* < *b* < *a*. (Since *a**n* − *b**n* is always divisible by *a* − *b*, the division is necessary for there to be any chance of finding prime numbers.)[[a]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-39) We can ask which n makes this number prime. It can be shown that such n must be primes themselves or equal to 4, and n can be 4 if and only if *a* + *b* = 1 and *a*2 + *b*2 is prime.[[b]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-40) It is a conjecture that for any pair (*a*, *b*) such that a and b are not both perfect rth powers for any r and −4*ab* is not a perfect [fourth power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_power), there are infinitely many values of n such that ⁠*a**n* − *b**n*/*a* − *b*⁠ is prime.[[c]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-41) However, this has not been proved for any single value of (*a*, *b*).

In [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics), a **Mersenne prime** is a [prime number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number) that is one less than a [power of two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_of_two). That is, it is a prime number [of the form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Of_the_form) *Mn* = 2*n* − 1 for some [integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer) *n*. They are named after [Marin Mersenne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marin_Mersenne), a French [Minim friar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minims_(religious_order)), who studied them in the early 17th century. If *n* is a [composite number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composite_number) then so is 2*n* − 1. Therefore, an equivalent definition of the Mersenne primes is that they are the prime numbers of the form *Mp* = 2*p* − 1 for some prime *p*.

The [exponents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponentiation) *n* that give Mersenne primes are 2, 3, 5, 7, 13, 17, 19, 31, ... (sequence [A000043](https://oeis.org/A000043) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences)) and the resulting Mersenne primes are [3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3_(number)), [7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/7_(number)), [31](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/31_(number)), [127](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/127_(number)), [8191](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/8191_(number)), 131071, 524287, [2147483647](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2,147,483,647), ... (sequence [A000668](https://oeis.org/A000668) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences)).

Numbers of the form *Mn* = 2*n* − 1 without the primality requirement may be called **Mersenne numbers**. Sometimes, however, Mersenne numbers are defined to have the additional requirement that *n* should be prime. The smallest composite Mersenne number with prime exponent *n* is 211 − 1 = 2047 = 23 × 89.

Mersenne primes were studied in antiquity because of their close [connection to perfect numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#Perfect_numbers): the [Euclid–Euler theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid%E2%80%93Euler_theorem) asserts a one-to-one correspondence between even perfect numbers and Mersenne primes. Many of the largest known primes are Mersenne primes because Mersenne numbers are easier to check for primality.

As of 2025[[ref]](http://primes.utm.edu/top20/page.php?id=4), [52 Mersenne primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Mersenne_primes_and_perfect_numbers) are known. The [largest known prime number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Largest_known_prime_number), 2136279841 − 1, is a Mersenne prime.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-GIMPS-2024-1)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-GIMPS-2018-2) Since 1997, all newly found Mersenne primes have been discovered by the [Great Internet Mersenne Prime Search](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Internet_Mersenne_Prime_Search), a [distributed computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributed_computing) project. In December 2020, a major milestone in the project was passed after all exponents below 100 million were checked at least once.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-3)

About Mersenne primes

# About Mersenne primes

A basic [theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theorem) about Mersenne numbers states that if Mp is prime, then the exponent *p* must also be prime. This follows from the identity

          2  a b   − 1    = (  2  a   − 1 ) ⋅  (  1 +  2  a   +  2  2 a   +  2  3 a   + ⋯ +  2  ( b − 1 ) a    )        = (  2  b   − 1 ) ⋅  (  1 +  2  b   +  2  2 b   +  2  3 b   + ⋯ +  2  ( a − 1 ) b    )  .       {\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}2^{ab}-1&=(2^{a}-1)\cdot \left(1+2^{a}+2^{2a}+2^{3a}+\cdots +2^{(b-1)a}\right)\\&=(2^{b}-1)\cdot \left(1+2^{b}+2^{2b}+2^{3b}+\cdots +2^{(a-1)b}\right).\end{aligned}}}

This rules out primality for Mersenne numbers with a composite exponent, such as *M*4 = 24 − 1 = 15 = 3 × 5 = (22 − 1) × (1 + 22).

Though the above examples might suggest that Mp is prime for all primes p, this is not the case, and the smallest counterexample is the Mersenne number

 *M*11 = 211 − 1 = 2047 = 23 × 89.  Unsolved problem in mathematics Are there infinitely many Mersenne primes? [More unsolved problems in mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_unsolved_problems_in_mathematics)

Many fundamental questions about Mersenne primes remain unresolved. It is not even known whether the set of Mersenne primes is finite or infinite.

The [Lenstra–Pomerance–Wagstaff conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenstra%E2%80%93Pomerance%E2%80%93Wagstaff_conjecture) claims that there are infinitely many Mersenne primes and predicts their [order of growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_analysis) and frequency: For every number n, there should on average be about      e  γ   ⋅  log  2   ⁡ ( 10 ) ≈ 5.92   {\displaystyle e^{\gamma }\cdot \log _{2}(10)\approx 5.92}   primes p with n decimal digits for which *M**p* is prime. Here, γ is the [Euler–Mascheroni constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler%E2%80%93Mascheroni_constant).

It is also not known whether infinitely many Mersenne numbers with prime exponents are composite, although this would follow from widely believed [conjectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjecture) about prime numbers; for example, from the infinitude of [Sophie Germain primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophie_Germain_prime) [congruent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congruence_relation) to 3 ([mod 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_arithmetic)). For these primes p, 2*p* + 1 (which is also prime) will divide Mp, for example, 23 | *M*11, 47 | *M*23, 167 | *M*83, 263 | *M*131, 359 | *M*179, 383 | *M*191, 479 | *M*239, and 503 | *M*251 (sequence [A002515](https://oeis.org/A002515) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences)). For these primes p, 2*p* + 1 is congruent to 7 mod 8, so 2 is a [quadratic residue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_residue) mod 2*p* + 1, and the [multiplicative order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_order) of 2 mod 2*p* + 1 must divide        ( 2 p + 1 ) − 1  2   = p   {\textstyle {\frac {(2p+1)-1}{2}}=p}  . Since p is a prime, it must be p or 1. However, it cannot be 1 since Φ1(2) = 1 and 1 has no [prime factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factor), so it must be p. Hence, 2*p* + 1 divides Φ*p*(2) = 2*p* − 1 and 2*p* − 1 = *M**p* cannot be prime. The first four Mersenne primes are *M*2 = 3, *M*3 = 7, *M*5 = 31 and *M*7 = 127 and because the first Mersenne prime starts at *M*2, all Mersenne primes are congruent to 3 (mod 4). Other than *M*0 = 0 and *M*1 = 1, all other Mersenne numbers are also congruent to 3 (mod 4). Consequently, in the [prime factorization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factorization) of a Mersenne number ( ≥ *M*2) there must be at least one prime factor congruent to 3 (mod 4).

The evidence at hand suggests that a randomly selected Mersenne number is much more likely to be prime than an arbitrary randomly selected odd integer of similar size.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-Wagstaff-4) Nonetheless, prime values of Mp appear to grow increasingly sparse as p increases. For example, eight of the first 11 primes p give rise to a Mersenne prime Mp (the correct terms on Mersenne's original list), while Mp is prime for only 43 of the first two million prime numbers (up to 32452843).

Since Mersenne numbers grow very rapidly, the search for Mersenne primes is a difficult task, even though there is a simple efficient test to determine whether a given Mersenne number is prime: the [Lucas–Lehmer primality test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucas%E2%80%93Lehmer_primality_test) (LLT), which makes it much easier to test the primality of Mersenne numbers than that of most other numbers of the same size. The search for the largest known prime has somewhat of a [cult following](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cult_following).[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)*] Consequently, a large amount of computer power has been expended searching for new Mersenne primes, much of which is now done using [distributed computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributed_computing).

Arithmetic modulo a Mersenne number is particularly efficient on a [binary computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_number), making them popular choices when a prime modulus is desired, such as the [Park–Miller random number generator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Park%E2%80%93Miller_random_number_generator). To find a [primitive polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_polynomial_(field_theory)) of Mersenne number order requires knowing the factorization of that number, so Mersenne primes allow one to find primitive polynomials of very high order. Such [primitive trinomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_polynomial_(field_theory)#Primitive_trinomials) are used in [pseudorandom number generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudorandom_number_generator) with very large periods such as the [Mersenne twister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_twister), generalized shift register and [Lagged Fibonacci generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagged_Fibonacci_generator).

Perfect numbers

# Perfect numbers

Main article: [Euclid–Euler theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid%E2%80%93Euler_theorem)

Mersenne primes *M**p* are closely connected to [perfect numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_number). In the 4th century BC, [Euclid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid) proved that if 2*p* − 1 is prime, then 2*p* − 1(2*p* − 1) is a perfect number. In the 18th century, [Leonhard Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonhard_Euler) proved that, conversely, all even perfect numbers have this form.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-5) This is known as the [Euclid–Euler theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid%E2%80%93Euler_theorem). It is unknown whether there are any [odd perfect numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_perfect_number).

Mersenne primes take their name from the 17th-century [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_people) scholar [Marin Mersenne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marin_Mersenne), who compiled what was supposed to be a list of Mersenne primes with exponents up to 257. The exponents listed by Mersenne in 1644 were as follows:

 2, 3, 5, 7, 13, 17, 19, 31, 67, 127, 257.

His list replicated the known primes of his time with exponents up to 19. His next entry, 31, was correct, but the list then became largely incorrect, as Mersenne mistakenly included *M*67 and *M*257 (which are composite) and omitted *M*61, *M*89, and *M*107 (which are prime). Mersenne gave little indication of how he came up with his list.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-6)

[Édouard Lucas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89douard_Lucas) proved in 1876 that *M*127 is indeed prime, as Mersenne claimed. This was the largest known prime number for 75 years until 1951, when Aimé Ferrier found a larger prime, (2148 + 1)/17, using a desk calculating machine.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-7): page 22  *M*61 was determined to be prime in 1883 by [Ivan Mikheevich Pervushin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Mikheevich_Pervushin), though Mersenne claimed it was composite, and for this reason it is sometimes called Pervushin's number.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)*] This was the second-largest known prime number, and it remained so until 1911. Lucas had shown another error in Mersenne's list in 1876 by demonstrating that *M*67 was composite without finding a factor. No factor was found until a famous talk by [Frank Nelson Cole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Nelson_Cole) in 1903.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-8) Without speaking a word, he went to a blackboard and raised 2 to the 67th power, then subtracted one, resulting in the number 147573952589676412927. On the other side of the board, he multiplied 193707721 × 761838257287 and got the same number, then returned to his seat (to applause) without speaking.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-9) He later said that the result had taken him "three years of Sundays" to find.[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-10) A correct list of all Mersenne primes in this number range was completed and rigorously verified only about three centuries after Mersenne published his list.

Searching for Mersenne primes

# Searching for Mersenne primes

Fast algorithms for finding Mersenne primes are available, and as of October 2024[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Mersenne_prime&action=edit), the seven [largest known prime numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Largest_known_prime_number) are Mersenne primes.

The first four Mersenne primes *M*2 = 3, *M*3 = 7, *M*5 = 31 and *M*7 = 127 were known in antiquity. The fifth, *M*13 = 8191, was discovered anonymously before 1461; the next two (*M*17 and *M*19) were found by [Pietro Cataldi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pietro_Cataldi) in 1588. After nearly two centuries, *M*31 was verified to be prime by [Leonhard Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonhard_Euler) in 1772. The next (in historical, not numerical order) was *M*127, found by [Édouard Lucas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89douard_Lucas) in 1876, then *M*61 by [Ivan Mikheevich Pervushin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Mikheevich_Pervushin) in 1883. Two more (*M*89 and *M*107) were found early in the 20th century, by [R. E. Powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._E._Powers) in 1911 and 1914, respectively.[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-11)

The most efficient method presently known for testing the primality of Mersenne numbers is the [Lucas–Lehmer primality test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucas%E2%80%93Lehmer_primality_test). Specifically, it can be shown that for prime *p* > 2, *Mp* = 2*p* − 1 is prime [if and only if](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/If_and_only_if) *Mp* divides *S**p* − 2, where *S*0 = 4 and *Sk* = (*S**k* − 1)2 − 2 for *k* > 0.

During the era of manual calculation, all previously untested exponents up to and including 257 were tested with the Lucas–Lehmer test and found to be composite. A notable contribution was made by retired Yale physics professor Horace Scudder Uhler, who did the calculations for exponents 157, 167, 193, 199, 227, and 229.[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-12) Unfortunately for those investigators, the interval they were testing contains the largest known relative gap between Mersenne primes: the next Mersenne prime exponent, 521, would turn out to be more than four times as large as the previous record of 127.

The search for Mersenne primes was revolutionized by the introduction of the electronic digital computer. [Alan Turing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Turing) searched for them on the [Manchester Mark 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Mark_1) in 1949,[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-13) but the first successful identification of a Mersenne prime, *M*521, by this means was achieved at 10:00 pm on January 30, 1952, using the U.S. [National Bureau of Standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bureau_of_Standards) [Western Automatic Computer (SWAC)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SWAC_(computer)) at the Institute for Numerical Analysis at the [University of California, Los Angeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Los_Angeles) (UCLA), under the direction of [D. H. Lehmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derrick_Henry_Lehmer), with a computer search program written and run by Prof. [R. M. Robinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raphael_M._Robinson). It was the first Mersenne prime to be identified in thirty-eight years; the next one, *M*607, was found by the computer a little less than two hours later. Three more – *M*1279, *M*2203, and *M*2281 – were found by the same program in the next several months. *M*4423 was the first prime discovered with more than 1000 digits, *M*44497 was the first with more than 10000, and *M*6972593 was the first with more than a million. In general, the number of digits in the decimal representation of *Mn* equals ⌊*n* log102⌋ + 1, where ⌊*x*⌋ denotes the [floor function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floor_function) (or equivalently ⌊log10*Mn*⌋ + 1).

In September 2008, mathematicians at UCLA participating in the [Great Internet Mersenne Prime Search](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Internet_Mersenne_Prime_Search) (GIMPS) won part of a $100000 prize from the [Electronic Frontier Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Frontier_Foundation) for their discovery of a very nearly 13-million-digit Mersenne prime. The prize, finally confirmed in October 2009, is for the first known prime with at least 10 million digits. The prime was found on a [Dell OptiPlex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dell_OptiPlex) 745 on August 23, 2008. This was the eighth Mersenne prime discovered at UCLA.[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-14)

On April 12, 2009, a GIMPS server log reported that a 47th Mersenne prime had possibly been found. The find was first noticed on June 4, 2009, and verified a week later. The prime is 242643801 − 1. Although it is chronologically the 47th Mersenne prime to be discovered, it is smaller than the largest known at the time, which was the 45th to be discovered.

On January 25, 2013, [Curtis Cooper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtis_Cooper_(mathematician)), a mathematician at the [University of Central Missouri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Central_Missouri), discovered a 48th Mersenne prime, 257885161 − 1 (a number with 17425170 digits), as a result of a search executed by a GIMPS server network.[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-15)

On January 19, 2016, Cooper published his discovery of a 49th Mersenne prime, 274207281 − 1 (a number with 22338618 digits), as a result of a search executed by a GIMPS server network.[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-GIMPS-2016-16)[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-17)[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-NYT-20160121-18) This was the fourth Mersenne prime discovered by Cooper and his team in the past ten years.

On September 2, 2016, the Great Internet Mersenne Prime Search finished verifying all tests below *M*37156667, thus officially confirming its position as the 45th Mersenne prime.[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-19)

On January 3, 2018, it was announced that Jonathan Pace, a 51-year-old electrical engineer living in [Germantown, Tennessee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germantown,_Tennessee), had found a 50th Mersenne prime, 277232917 − 1 (a number with 23249425 digits), as a result of a search executed by a GIMPS server network.[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-20) The discovery was made by a computer in the offices of a church in the same town.[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-21)[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-22)

On December 21, 2018, it was announced that The Great Internet Mersenne Prime Search (GIMPS) discovered a new prime number, 282589933 − 1, having 24862048 digits. A computer volunteered by Patrick Laroche from [Ocala, Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocala,_Florida) made the find on December 7, 2018.[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-23)

In late 2020, GIMPS began using a new technique to rule out potential Mersenne primes called the [Probable prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probable_prime) (PRP) test, based on development from Robert Gerbicz in 2017, and a simple way to verify tests developed by Krzysztof Pietrzak in 2018. Due to the low error rate and ease of proof, this nearly halved the computing time to rule out potential primes over the Lucas-Lehmer test (as two users would no longer have to perform the same test to confirm the other's result), although exponents passing the PRP test still require at least two Lucas-Lehmer tests to confirm their primality.[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-24)

On October 12, 2024, a user named Luke Durant from San Jose, California, found the current largest known Mersenne prime, 2136279841 − 1, having 41024320 digits. This marks the first Mersenne prime with an exponent over 100000000. This was announced on October 21, 2024.[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-25)

Theorems about Mersenne numbers

# Theorems about Mersenne numbers

Mersenne numbers are 0, 1, 3, 7, 15, 31, 63, ... (sequence [A000225](https://oeis.org/A000225) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences)).

1. If *a* and p are natural numbers such that *ap* − 1 is prime, then *a* = 2 or *p* = 1. **Proof**: *a* ≡ 1 ([mod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_arithmetic) *a* − 1). Then *ap* ≡ 1 (mod *a* − 1), so *ap* − 1 ≡ 0 (mod *a* − 1). Thus *a* − 1 | *ap* − 1. However, *ap* − 1 is prime, so *a* − 1 = *ap* − 1 or *a* − 1 = ±1. In the former case, *a* = *ap*, hence *a* = 0, 1 (which is a contradiction, as neither −1 nor 0 is prime) or *p* = 1. In the latter case, *a* = 2 or *a* = 0. If *a* = 0, however, 0*p* − 1 = 0 − 1 = −1, which is not prime. Therefore, *a* = 2.
2. If 2*p* − 1 is prime, then p is prime. **Proof**: Suppose that p is composite, hence can be written *p* = *ab* with a and *b* > 1. Then 2*p* − 1 = 2*ab* − 1 = (2*a*)*b* − 1 = (2*a* − 1)((2*a*)*b*−1 + (2*a*)*b*−2 + ... + 2*a* + 1) so 2*p* − 1 is composite. By contraposition, if 2*p* − 1 is prime then *p* is prime.
3. If p is an odd prime, then every prime q that divides 2*p* − 1 must be 1 plus a multiple of 2*p*. This holds even when 2*p* − 1 is prime. For example, 25 − 1 = 31 is prime, and 31 = 1 + 3 × (2 × 5). A composite example is 211 − 1 = 23 × 89, where 23 = 1 + (2 × 11) and 89 = 1 + 4 × (2 × 11). **Proof**: By [Fermat's little theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat's_little_theorem), q is a factor of 2*q*−1 − 1. Since q is a factor of 2*p* − 1, for all positive integers *c*, q is also a factor of 2*pc* − 1. Since p is prime and q is not a factor of 21 − 1, p is also the smallest positive integer x such that q is a factor of 2*x* − 1. As a result, for all positive integers x, q is a factor of 2*x* − 1 if and only if p is a factor of x. Therefore, since q is a factor of 2*q*−1 − 1, p is a factor of *q* − 1 so *q* ≡ 1 (mod *p*). Furthermore, since q is a factor of 2*p* − 1, which is odd, q is odd. Therefore, *q* ≡ 1 (mod 2*p*). This fact leads to a proof of [Euclid's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid's_theorem), which asserts the infinitude of primes, distinct from the proof written by Euclid: for every odd prime p, all primes dividing 2*p* − 1 are larger than p; thus there are always larger primes than any particular prime. It follows from this fact that for every prime *p* > 2, there is at least one prime of the form 2*kp* + 1 less than or equal to Mp, for some integer k.
4. If p is an odd prime, then every prime q that divides 2*p* − 1 is congruent to ±1 (mod 8). **Proof**: 2*p*+1 ≡ 2 (mod *q*), so 2⁠1/2⁠(p+1) is a square root of 2 mod *q*. By [quadratic reciprocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_reciprocity), every prime modulus in which the number 2 has a square root is congruent to ±1 (mod 8).
5. A Mersenne prime cannot be a [Wieferich prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wieferich_prime). **Proof**: We show if *p* = 2*m* − 1 is a Mersenne prime, then the congruence 2*p*−1 ≡ 1 (mod *p*2) does not hold. By Fermat's little theorem, *m* | *p* − 1. Therefore, one can write *p* − 1 = *mλ*. If the given congruence is satisfied, then *p*2 | 2*mλ* − 1, therefore 0 ≡ ⁠2*mλ* − 1/2*m* − 1⁠ = 1 + 2*m* + 22*m* + ... + 2(*λ* − 1)*m* ≡ *λ* mod (2*m* − 1). Hence p | *λ*, and therefore −1 = 0 (mod p), which is impossible.
6. If m and n are natural numbers then m and n are [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) if and only if 2*m* − 1 and 2*n* − 1 are coprime. Consequently, a prime number divides at most one prime-exponent Mersenne number.[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-26) That is, the set of [pernicious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pernicious_number) Mersenne numbers is pairwise coprime.
7. If p and 2*p* + 1 are both prime (meaning that p is a [Sophie Germain prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophie_Germain_prime)), and p is [congruent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congruence_relation) to 3 (mod 4), then 2*p* + 1 divides 2*p* − 1.[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-27) **Example**: 11 and 23 are both prime, and 11 = 2 × 4 + 3, so 23 divides 211 − 1. **Proof**: Let q be 2*p* + 1. By Fermat's little theorem, 22*p* ≡ 1 (mod *q*), so either 2*p* ≡ 1 (mod *q*) or 2*p* ≡ −1 (mod *q*). Supposing latter true, then 2*p*+1 = (2⁠1/2⁠(*p* + 1))2 ≡ −2 (mod *q*), so −2 would be a quadratic residue mod q. However, since p is congruent to 3 (mod 4), q is congruent to 7 (mod 8) and therefore 2 is a quadratic residue mod q. Also since q is congruent to 3 (mod 4), −1 is a quadratic nonresidue mod q, so −2 is the product of a residue and a nonresidue and hence it is a nonresidue, which is a contradiction. Hence, the former congruence must be true and 2*p* + 1 divides Mp.
8. All composite divisors of prime-exponent Mersenne numbers are [strong pseudoprimes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_pseudoprime) to the base 2.
9. With the exception of 1, a Mersenne number cannot be a perfect power. That is, and in accordance with [Mihăilescu's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalan's_conjecture), the equation 2*m* − 1 = *nk* has no solutions where m, n, and k are integers with *m* > 1 and *k* > 1.
10. The Mersenne number sequence is a member of the family of [Lucas sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucas_sequence). It is U*n*(3, 2). That is, Mersenne number *m**n* = 3*m**n*−1 − 2*m**n*−2 with *m*0 = 0 and *m*1 = 1.

Factorization of composite Mersenne numbers

# Factorization of composite Mersenne numbers

Since they are prime numbers, Mersenne primes are divisible only by 1 and themselves. However, not all Mersenne numbers are Mersenne primes. Mersenne numbers are very good test cases for the [special number field sieve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_number_field_sieve) algorithm, so often the largest number factorized with this algorithm has been a Mersenne number. As of June 2019[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Mersenne_prime&action=edit), 21193 − 1 is the record-holder,[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-28) having been factored with a variant of the special number field sieve that allows the factorization of several numbers at once. See *[Integer factorization records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer_factorization_records)* for links to more information. The special number field sieve can factorize numbers with more than one large factor. If a number has only one very large factor then other algorithms can factorize larger numbers by first finding small factors and then running a [primality test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primality_test) on the cofactor. As of September 2022[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Mersenne_prime&action=edit), the largest completely factored number (with [probable prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probable_prime) factors allowed) is 212720787 − 1 = 1119429257 × 175573124547437977 × 8480999878421106991 × *q*, where q is a 3829294-digit probable prime. It was discovered by a GIMPS participant with nickname "Funky Waddle".[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-29)[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-30) As of September 2026[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Mersenne_prime&action=edit), the Mersenne number *M*1619 is the smallest composite Mersenne number with no known factors.

Mersenne numbers in nature and elsewhere

# Mersenne numbers in nature and elsewhere

In the mathematical problem [Tower of Hanoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_of_Hanoi), optimally solving a puzzle with an *n*-disc tower requires *Mn* steps.[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-31) The number of rice grains on the whole chessboard in the [wheat and chessboard problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheat_and_chessboard_problem) is *M*64.[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-32)

The [asteroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid) with [minor planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_planet) number 8191 is named [8191 Mersenne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/8191_Mersenne) after Marin Mersenne, because 8191 is a Mersenne prime.[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-33)

In [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry), an integer [right triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_triangle) that is [primitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_triple) and has its even leg a power of 2 ( ≥ 4) generates a unique right triangle such that its [inradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inradius) is always a Mersenne number. For example, if the even leg is 2*n* + 1 then because it is primitive it constrains the odd leg to be 4*n* − 1, the [hypotenuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypotenuse) to be 4*n* + 1 and its inradius to be 2*n* − 1.[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-34)

Also in geometry, the number of [polytopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytopes) that are part of the family of polytopes formed by a [truncation operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_polytope#Operations) of a base regular polytope and its [dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_polyhedron#Dual_polytopes_and_tessellations) (excluding alternation) is equal to *Mn* where n is the dimension of the base polytope. For example, a [tesseract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesseract) and its dual the [hexadecachoron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexadecachoron) have *M4* = 15 different polytopes in its family formed by truncation operations.[[35]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-35)

Mersenne–Fermat primes

# Mersenne–Fermat primes

A **Mersenne–Fermat number** is defined as ⁠2*pr* − 1/2*p**r* − 1 − 1⁠ with *p* prime, *r* natural number, and can be written as MF(*p*, *r*). When *r* = 1, it is a Mersenne number. When *p* = 2, it is a [Fermat number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat_number). The only known Mersenne–Fermat primes with *r* > 1 are

 MF(2, 2), MF(2, 3), MF(2, 4), MF(2, 5), MF(3, 2), MF(3, 3), MF(7, 2), and MF(59, 2).[[36]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-36)

In fact, MF(*p*, *r*) = *Φpr*(2), where *Φ* is the [cyclotomic polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclotomic_polynomial).

Generalizations

# Generalizations

Main article: [Generalized Mersenne prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_Mersenne_prime)

The simplest generalized Mersenne primes are prime numbers of the form *f*(2*n*), where *f*(*x*) is a low-degree [polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial) with small integer [coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coefficients).[[37]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-37) An example is 264 − 232 + 1, in this case, *n* = 32, and *f*(*x*) = *x*2 − *x* + 1; another example is 2192 − 264 − 1, in this case, *n* = 64, and *f*(*x*) = *x*3 − *x* − 1.

It is also natural to try to generalize primes of the form 2*n* − 1 to primes of the form *b**n* − 1 (for *b* ≠ 2 and *n* > 1). However (see also *[§ Theorems about Mersenne numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#Theorems_about_Mersenne_numbers)* above), *b**n* − 1 is always divisible by *b* − 1, so unless the latter is a [unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_(ring_theory)), the former is not a prime. Complex numbers

# Complex numbers

In the [ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_(mathematics)) of integers (on [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_number)), if *b* − 1 is a [unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_(ring_theory)), then *b* is either 2 or 0. But 2*n* − 1 are the usual Mersenne primes, and the formula 0*n* − 1 does not lead to anything interesting (since it is always −1 for all *n* > 0). Thus, we can regard a ring of "integers" on [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_number) instead of [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_number), like [Gaussian integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_integer) and [Eisenstein integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eisenstein_integer).

#### Gaussian Mersenne primes

If we regard the ring of [Gaussian integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_integer), we get the case *b* = 1 + *i* and *b* = 1 − *i*, and can ask ([WLOG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Without_loss_of_generality)) for which *n* the number (1 + *i*)*n* − 1 is a [Gaussian prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_prime), which will then be called a **Gaussian Mersenne prime**.[[38]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-38)

(1 + *i*)*n* − 1 is a Gaussian prime for the following *n*:

 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 19, 29, 47, 73, 79, 113, 151, 157, 163, 167, 239, 241, 283, 353, 367, 379, 457, 997, 1367, 3041, 10141, 14699, 27529, 49207, 77291, 85237, 106693, 160423, 203789, 364289, 991961, 1203793, 1667321, 3704053, 4792057, ... (sequence [A057429](https://oeis.org/A057429) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences))

Like the sequence of exponents for usual Mersenne primes, this sequence contains only (rational) prime numbers.

As for all Gaussian primes, the [norms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_integer#Norm_of_a_Gaussian_integer) (that is, squares of absolute values) of these numbers are rational primes:

 5, 13, 41, 113, 2113, 525313, 536903681, 140737471578113, ... (sequence [A182300](https://oeis.org/A182300) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences)).

#### Eisenstein Mersenne primes

One may encounter cases where such a Mersenne prime is also an *Eisenstein prime*, being of the form *b* = 1 + *ω* and *b* = 1 − *ω*. In these cases, such numbers are called **Eisenstein Mersenne primes**.

(1 + *ω*)*n* − 1 is an Eisenstein prime for the following *n*:

The norms (that is, squares of absolute values) of these Eisenstein primes are rational primes:

#### Other generalized Mersenne primes

Another generalized Mersenne number is

         a  n   −  b  n     a − b      {\displaystyle {\frac {a^{n}-b^{n}}{a-b}}}

with a, b any [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) integers, *a* > 1 and −*a* < *b* < *a*. (Since *a**n* − *b**n* is always divisible by *a* − *b*, the division is necessary for there to be any chance of finding prime numbers.)[[a]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-39) We can ask which n makes this number prime. It can be shown that such n must be primes themselves or equal to 4, and n can be 4 if and only if *a* + *b* = 1 and *a*2 + *b*2 is prime.[[b]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-40) It is a conjecture that for any pair (*a*, *b*) such that a and b are not both perfect rth powers for any r and −4*ab* is not a perfect [fourth power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_power), there are infinitely many values of n such that ⁠*a**n* − *b**n*/*a* − *b*⁠ is prime.[[c]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_note-41) However, this has not been proved for any single value of (*a*, *b*).

# Notes

1. [↑](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_ref-39) This number is the same as the [Lucas number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucas_sequence) *Un*(*a* + *b*, *ab*), since a and b are the [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_of_a_function) of the [quadratic equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_equation) *x*2 − (*a* + *b*)*x* + *ab* = 0.
2. [↑](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_ref-40) Since ⁠*a*4 − *b*4/*a* − *b*⁠ = (*a* + *b*)(*a*2 + *b*2). Thus, in this case the pair (*a*, *b*) must be (*x* + 1, −*x*) and *x*2 + (*x* + 1)2 must be prime. That is, x must be in (sequence [A027861](https://oeis.org/A027861) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences)).
3. [↑](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime#cite_ref-41) When a and b are both perfect rth powers for some *r* > 1 or when −4*ab* is a perfect fourth power, it can be shown that there are at most two values of n with this property: in these cases, ⁠*a**n* − *b**n*/*a* − *b*⁠ can be factored algebraically.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)*]
