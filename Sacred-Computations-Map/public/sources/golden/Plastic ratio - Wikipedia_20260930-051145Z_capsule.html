In mathematics, the **plastic ratio** is a geometrical [proportion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspect_ratio), given by the unique real [solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_root) of the equation *x*3 = *x* + 1. Its decimal expansion begins with 1.324717957244746...  (sequence [A060006](https://oeis.org/A060006) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences)). It is the smallest [Pisot number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisot_number).

The adjective *plastic* does not refer to [the artificial material](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic), but to the formative and sculptural qualities of this ratio, as in *plastic arts*.

## Van der Laan sequence

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Plastic_ratio&action=edit&section=3)] [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d8/Plastic5_Rauzy_sqr.png/330px-Plastic5_Rauzy_sqr.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Plastic5_Rauzy_sqr.png)A fan of plastic Rauzy tiles with areas in ratio Ⴔ. In his quest for perceptible clarity, the Dutch [Benedictine monk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedictine_monk) and architect Dom [Hans van der Laan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_van_der_Laan) (1904-1991) asked for the minimum difference between two sizes, so that we will clearly perceive them as distinct. Also, what is the maximum ratio of two sizes, so that we can still relate them and perceive nearness. According to his observations, the answers are 1/4 and 7/1, spanning a single *order of size*.[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-12) Requiring proportional continuity, he constructed a [geometric series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_series) of [eight measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#types) (*types of size*) with common ratio 2 / (3/4 + 1/71/7) ≈ ρ. Put in rational form, this architectonic system of measure is constructed from a subset of the numbers that bear his name.

The Van der Laan numbers have a close connection to the [Perrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perrin_number) and [Padovan sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Padovan_sequence). In combinatorics, the number of [compositions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composition_(combinatorics)) of n into parts 2 and 3 is counted by the *n*th Van der Laan number.

The Van der Laan sequence is defined by the third-order [recurrence relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recurrence_relation)      V  n   =  V  n − 2   +  V  n − 3     for   n > 2 ,   {\displaystyle V_{n}=V_{n-2}+V_{n-3}{\text{ for }}n>2,}   with initial values      V  1   = 0 ,  V  0   =  V  2   = 1.   {\displaystyle V_{1}=0,V_{0}=V_{2}=1.}

The first few terms are 1, 0, 1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 9, 12, 16, 21, 28, 37, 49, 65, 86,... (sequence [A182097](https://oeis.org/A182097) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences)). The limit ratio between consecutive terms is the plastic ratio:     lim  n → ∞    V  n + 1    /   V  n   = ρ .   {\displaystyle \lim _{n\rightarrow \infty }V_{n+1}/V_{n}=\rho .}

The [generating function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generating_function) of the Van der Laan sequence for non-negative ⁠    n   {\displaystyle n}  ⁠ is given by [[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-oeisGF-14)       1  1 −  x  2   −  x  3      =  ∑  n = 0   ∞    V  n    x  n     for   x <    1 ρ      {\displaystyle {\frac {1}{1-x^{2}-x^{3}}}=\sum _{n=0}^{\infty }V_{n}x^{n}{\text{ for }}x<{\tfrac {1}{\rho }}}

The sequence is related to sums of [binomial coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_coefficient) by [[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-oeispado-15)      V  n   =  ∑  k = ⌊ ( n + 2 )  /  3 ⌋   ⌊ n  /  2 ⌋      (   k  n − 2 k    )      {\displaystyle V_{n}=\sum _{k=\lfloor (n+2)/3\rfloor }^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor }{k \choose n-2k}}

The [characteristic equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_equation_(calculus)) of the recurrence is      x  3   − x − 1 = 0.   {\displaystyle x^{3}-x-1=0.}   If the three solutions are real root ⁠    ρ   {\displaystyle \rho }  ⁠ and conjugate pair ⁠    β   {\displaystyle \beta }  ⁠ and ⁠    γ   {\displaystyle \gamma }  ⁠, the Van der Laan numbers are given by the [Binet formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_sequence#Binet's_formula) [[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-oeispado-15)      V  n − 1   = a  ρ  n   + b  β  n   + c  γ  n   ,   {\displaystyle V_{n-1}=a\rho ^{n}+b\beta ^{n}+c\gamma ^{n},}   with real ⁠    a   {\displaystyle a}  ⁠ and conjugates ⁠    b   {\displaystyle b}  ⁠ and ⁠    c   {\displaystyle c}  ⁠ the roots of     23  x  3   + x − 1 = 0.   {\displaystyle 23x^{3}+x-1=0.}

Since      |  b  β  n   + c  γ  n    |  <  ρ  − n  /  2     {\displaystyle \left\vert b\beta ^{n}+c\gamma ^{n}\right\vert <\rho ^{-n/2}}  , the number ⁠     V  n     {\displaystyle V_{n}}  ⁠ is the [nearest integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nearest_integer_function) to ⁠     a   ρ  n + 1     {\displaystyle \,a\,\rho ^{n+1}}  ⁠, with ⁠    n > 1   {\displaystyle n>1}  ⁠ and coefficient     a =  ρ  2    /  (  ρ  3   + ρ + 2 ) =   {\displaystyle a=\rho ^{2}/(\rho ^{3}+\rho +2)=}   0.3106288296404670777619027...

Coefficients     a = b = c = 1   {\displaystyle a=b=c=1}   result in the Binet formula for the related sequence      P  n   = 2  V  n   +  V  n − 3   .   {\displaystyle P_{n}=2V_{n}+V_{n-3}.}

The first few terms are 3, 0, 2, 3, 2, 5, 5, 7, 10, 12, 17, 22, 29, 39, 51, 68, 90, 119,... (sequence [A001608](https://oeis.org/A001608) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences)).

This [Perrin sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perrin_sequence) has the [Fermat property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat's_little_theorem): if p is prime,      P  p   ≡  P  1    mod  p   .   {\displaystyle P_{p}\equiv P_{1}{\bmod {p}}.}   The converse does not hold, but the small number of [pseudoprimes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoprime)      n ∣  P  n     {\displaystyle \,n\mid P_{n}}   makes the sequence special.[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-16) The only 7 composite numbers below 108  to pass the test are n = 5212, 904631, 16532714, 24658561, 27422714, 27664033, 46672291.[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-17)

The Van der Laan numbers are obtained as integral powers *n* > 2 of a [matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_(mathematics)) with real [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues_and_eigenvectors#Eigenvalues_and_eigenvectors_of_matrices) ⁠    ρ   {\displaystyle \rho }  ⁠ [[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-oeisGF-14)     Q =   (    0   1   1     1   0   0     0   1   0    )   ,   {\displaystyle Q={\begin{pmatrix}0&1&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}},}

Q  n   =   (     V  n      V  n + 1      V  n − 1        V  n − 1      V  n      V  n − 2        V  n − 2      V  n − 1      V  n − 3      )     {\displaystyle Q^{n}={\begin{pmatrix}V_{n}&V_{n+1}&V_{n-1}\\V_{n-1}&V_{n}&V_{n-2}\\V_{n-2}&V_{n-1}&V_{n-3}\end{pmatrix}}}

The [trace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trace_(linear_algebra)#Relationship_to_eigenvalues) of ⁠     Q  n     {\displaystyle Q^{n}}  ⁠ gives the Perrin numbers.

Alternatively, ⁠    Q   {\displaystyle Q}  ⁠ can be interpreted as [incidence matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incidence_matrix) for a [D0L](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphic_word#D0L_system) [Lindenmayer system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L-system) on the alphabet ⁠    { a , b , c }   {\displaystyle \{a,b,c\}}  ⁠ with corresponding [substitution rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-Thue_system)       {    a  ↦  b     b  ↦  a c     c  ↦  a         {\displaystyle {\begin{cases}a\;\mapsto \;b\\b\;\mapsto \;ac\\c\;\mapsto \;a\end{cases}}}   and initiator ⁠     w  0   = c   {\displaystyle w_{0}=c}  ⁠. The series of words ⁠     w  n     {\displaystyle w_{n}}  ⁠ produced by iterating the substitution have lengths     l (  w  n   ) =  V  n + 2     {\displaystyle l(w_{n})=V_{n+2}}  . The number of *c*, *b* and *a's* in each word is equal to successive Van der Laan numbers. Associated to this string rewriting process is a set composed of three overlapping [self-similar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-similarity) tiles called the [Rauzy fractal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rauzy_fractal), that visualizes the [combinatorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorics_on_words) information contained in a multiple-generation letter sequence.[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-18)

The ratio between the number of digits in successive strings for the ternary version of the [look-and-say sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Look-and-say_sequence) converges to ⁠    ρ   {\displaystyle \rho }  ⁠.[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-19)[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-20)

### Partitioning the square

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Plastic_ratio&action=edit&section=5)] [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/48/Plastic_square_partitions.svg/500px-Plastic_square_partitions.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Plastic_square_partitions.svg)Three partitions of a square into similar rectangles, 1 = 3·⁠1/3⁠ = ⁠2/3⁠ + 2·⁠1/6⁠ = ⁠1/ρ2 ⁠ + ⁠1/ρ4 ⁠ + ⁠1/ρ8 ⁠.

There are precisely three ways of partitioning a square into three similar rectangles:[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-21)[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-22)

1. The trivial solution given by three congruent rectangles with aspect ratio 3:1.
2. The solution in which two of the three rectangles are congruent and the third one has twice the side lengths of the other two, where the rectangles have aspect ratio 3:2.
3. The solution in which the three rectangles are all of different sizes and where they have aspect ratio *ρ*2. The ratios of the linear sizes of the three rectangles are: *ρ* (large:medium); *ρ*2 (medium:small); and *ρ*3 (large:small). The internal, long edge of the largest rectangle (the square's fault line) divides two of the square's four edges into two segments each that stand to one another in the ratio *ρ.* The internal, coincident short edge of the medium rectangle and long edge of the small rectangle divides one of the square's other, two edges into two segments that stand to one another in the ratio *ρ*4.

The fact that a rectangle of aspect ratio *ρ*2 can be used for dissections of a square into similar rectangles is equivalent to an algebraic property of the number *ρ*2 related to the [Routh–Hurwitz theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Routh%E2%80%93Hurwitz_theorem): all of its conjugates have positive real part.[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-23)[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-24) [![Newton map for the rho\-squared polynomial\.](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4a/RhoSqr_Newton_map.svg/40px-RhoSqr_Newton_map.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:RhoSqr_Newton_map.svg)

### Plastic pentagon

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Plastic_ratio&action=edit&section=7)] [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5f/Plastic_pentagons.svg/330px-Plastic_pentagons.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Plastic_pentagons.svg)A [triskeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triskelion) of plastic pentagons.

A spiral of [equilateral triangles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilateral_triangle) with edges in ratio ⁠    ρ   {\displaystyle \rho }  ⁠ tiles a *plastic [pentagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentagon)* with four angles of 120 and one of 60 degrees.[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-29) The initial triangle is positioned at the left-hand side of a [parallelogram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallelogram) with base to side ratio ⁠    ρ   {\displaystyle \rho }  ⁠ and left base angle 60 degrees, so that two edges of the triangle are collinear with sides of the parallelogram. Scaling the parallelogram in ratio ⁠       1 ρ    ,   {\displaystyle {\tfrac {1}{\rho }},}  ⁠ accompanied with a clockwise rotation by 60 degrees, the horizontal base is mapped onto the third edge of the triangle. The [centre of rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_(mathematics)#Related_definitions_and_terminology) is on the short (falling) diagonal, dividing it in ratio ⁠     ρ  3     {\displaystyle \rho ^{3}}  ⁠, the expansion rate for a half-turn. Iteration of the process traces an infinite, closed sequence of equilateral triangles with pentagonal boundary.

The [logarithmic spiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithmic_spiral) through the vertices of all triangles has polar slope     k =   3 π   ln ⁡ ( ρ ) .   {\displaystyle k={\frac {3}{\pi }}\ln(\rho ).}   For parallelogram base ⁠    ρ   {\displaystyle \rho }  ⁠, the length of the short diagonal is        ρ  2   − ρ + 1     {\displaystyle {\sqrt {\rho ^{2}-\rho +1}}}   with angle     arctan ⁡ (     3   1 − 2 ρ     ) .   {\displaystyle \arctan({\tfrac {\sqrt {3}}{1-2\rho }}).}   The length of the discrete spiral is      ρ  5   =  ∑  n = 0   ∞    ρ  − n   ;   {\displaystyle \rho ^{5}=\sum _{n=0}^{\infty }\rho ^{-n};}   the pentagon has area         3  4     ρ  3   =     3  4     ∑  n = 0   ∞    ρ  − 2 n   .   {\displaystyle {\tfrac {\sqrt {3}}{4}}\rho ^{3}={\tfrac {\sqrt {3}}{4}}\sum _{n=0}^{\infty }\rho ^{-2n}.}

In the vector image, the construction is repeated on each side of a ⁠    ρ   {\displaystyle \rho }  ⁠ triangle. [John Rutherford Boyd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rutherford_Boyd) discovered a related figure, build on the sides of the ⁠     ρ  − 4     {\displaystyle \rho ^{-4}}  ⁠ triangle.[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-30) [![Spiral design by Rutherford Boyd\.](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d6/Plastic_pentagons_alt.svg/40px-Plastic_pentagons_alt.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Plastic_pentagons_alt.svg)

### Plastic spiral

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Plastic_ratio&action=edit&section=8)] [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/43/Plastic_spiral.svg/330px-Plastic_spiral.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Plastic_spiral.svg)Two plastic spirals with different initial radii.[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/83/Chambered_nautilus_shell_and_plastic_spiral.svg/330px-Chambered_nautilus_shell_and_plastic_spiral.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Chambered_nautilus_shell_and_plastic_spiral.svg)Chambered nautilus shell and plastic spiral.

A *plastic spiral* is a [logarithmic spiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithmic_spiral) that gets wider by a factor of ⁠    ρ   {\displaystyle \rho }  ⁠ for every quarter turn. It is described by the [polar equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_equation)     r ( θ ) = a exp ⁡ ( k θ ) ,   {\displaystyle r(\theta )=a\exp(k\theta ),}   with initial radius ⁠    a   {\displaystyle a}  ⁠ and parameter     k =   2 π   ln ⁡ ( ρ ) .   {\displaystyle k={\frac {2}{\pi }}\ln(\rho ).}   If drawn on a rectangle with sides in ratio ⁠    ρ   {\displaystyle \rho }  ⁠, the spiral has its pole at the foot of altitude of a triangle on the diagonal and passes through vertices of rectangles with aspect ratio ⁠     ρ  2     {\displaystyle \rho ^{2}}  ⁠ which are perpendicularly aligned and successively scaled by a factor ⁠     ρ  − 1   .   {\displaystyle \rho ^{-1}.}  ⁠

In 1838 [Henry Moseley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Moseley_(mathematician)) noticed that whorls of a shell of the [chambered nautilus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chambered_nautilus) are in geometrical progression: "It will be found that the distance of any two of its whorls measured upon a radius vector is *one-third* that of the next two whorls measured upon the same radius vector ... The curve is therefore a logarithmic spiral."[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-31) Moseley thus gave the expansion rate       3  4    ≈ ρ − 1  /  116   {\displaystyle {\sqrt[{4}]{3}}\approx \rho -1/116}   for a quarter turn.[[e]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-32) Considering the plastic ratio a three-dimensional equivalent of the ubiquitous golden ratio, it appears to be a natural candidate for measuring the shell.[[f]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-34)

## History and names

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Plastic_ratio&action=edit&section=10)]

ρ was first studied by [Axel Thue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axel_Thue) in 1912 and by [G. H. Hardy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._H._Hardy) in 1919.[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-35) French high school student [Gérard Cordonnier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%A9rard_Cordonnier?action=edit&redlink=1) [[fr](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%A9rard%20Cordonnier)] discovered the ratio for himself in 1924. In his correspon­dence with [Hans van der Laan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_van_der_Laan) a few years later, he called it the radiant number ([French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language): *le nombre radiant*). Van der Laan initially referred to it as the fundamental ratio ([Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_language): *de grondverhouding*), using the plastic number ([Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_language): *het plastische getal*) from the 1950s onward.[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVoet2016note_12-36) In 1944 [Carl Siegel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Ludwig_Siegel) showed that ρ is the smallest possible [Pisot–Vijayaraghavan number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number) and suggested naming it in honour of Thue.

 [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0d/Interieur_bovenkerk%2C_zicht_op_de_middenbeuk_met_koorbanken_voor_de_monniken_-_Mamelis_-_20536587_-_RCE.jpg/330px-Interieur_bovenkerk%2C_zicht_op_de_middenbeuk_met_koorbanken_voor_de_monniken_-_Mamelis_-_20536587_-_RCE.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Interieur_bovenkerk,_zicht_op_de_middenbeuk_met_koorbanken_voor_de_monniken_-_Mamelis_-_20536587_-_RCE.jpg)The 1967 St. Benedictusberg Abbey church designed by Hans van der Laan.

Unlike the names of the [golden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_ratio) and [silver ratios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_ratio), the word plastic was not intended by van der Laan to refer to a specific substance, but rather in its adjectival sense, meaning something that can be given a three-dimensional shape.[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-37) This, according to [Richard Padovan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Padovan), is because the characteristic ratios of the number, ⁠3/4⁠ and ⁠1/7⁠, relate to the limits of human perception in relating one physical size to another. Van der Laan designed the 1967 [St. Benedictusberg Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Benedictusberg_Abbey) church to these plastic number proportions.[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-38)

The plastic number is also sometimes called the silver number, a name given to it by [Midhat J. Gazalé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midhat_J._Gazal%C3%A9) [[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-39) and subsequently used by [Martin Gardner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Gardner),[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-40) but that name is more commonly used for the [silver ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_ratio) 1 + √2, one of the ratios from the family of [metallic means](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metallic_mean) first described by [Vera W. de Spinadel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vera_W._de_Spinadel). Gardner suggested referring to ρ2 as "high phi", and [Donald Knuth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Knuth) created a special typographic mark for this name, a variant of the Greek letter phi ("φ") with its central circle raised, resembling the Georgian letter [pari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pari_(letter)) ("Ⴔ").

In mathematics, the **plastic ratio** is a geometrical [proportion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspect_ratio), given by the unique real [solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_root) of the equation *x*3 = *x* + 1. Its decimal expansion begins with 1.324717957244746...  (sequence [A060006](https://oeis.org/A060006) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences)). It is the smallest [Pisot number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisot_number).

The adjective *plastic* does not refer to [the artificial material](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic), but to the formative and sculptural qualities of this ratio, as in *plastic arts*.

# Van der Laan sequence

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Plastic_ratio&action=edit&section=3)] [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d8/Plastic5_Rauzy_sqr.png/330px-Plastic5_Rauzy_sqr.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Plastic5_Rauzy_sqr.png)A fan of plastic Rauzy tiles with areas in ratio Ⴔ. In his quest for perceptible clarity, the Dutch [Benedictine monk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedictine_monk) and architect Dom [Hans van der Laan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_van_der_Laan) (1904-1991) asked for the minimum difference between two sizes, so that we will clearly perceive them as distinct. Also, what is the maximum ratio of two sizes, so that we can still relate them and perceive nearness. According to his observations, the answers are 1/4 and 7/1, spanning a single *order of size*.[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-12) Requiring proportional continuity, he constructed a [geometric series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_series) of [eight measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#types) (*types of size*) with common ratio 2 / (3/4 + 1/71/7) ≈ ρ. Put in rational form, this architectonic system of measure is constructed from a subset of the numbers that bear his name.

The Van der Laan numbers have a close connection to the [Perrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perrin_number) and [Padovan sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Padovan_sequence). In combinatorics, the number of [compositions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composition_(combinatorics)) of n into parts 2 and 3 is counted by the *n*th Van der Laan number.

The Van der Laan sequence is defined by the third-order [recurrence relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recurrence_relation)      V  n   =  V  n − 2   +  V  n − 3     for   n > 2 ,   {\displaystyle V_{n}=V_{n-2}+V_{n-3}{\text{ for }}n>2,}   with initial values      V  1   = 0 ,  V  0   =  V  2   = 1.   {\displaystyle V_{1}=0,V_{0}=V_{2}=1.}

(sequence [A182097](https://oeis.org/A182097) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences)). The [trace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trace_(linear_algebra)#Relationship_to_eigenvalues) of ⁠     Q  n     {\displaystyle Q^{n}}  ⁠ gives the Perrin numbers.

Alternatively, ⁠    Q   {\displaystyle Q}  ⁠ can be interpreted as [incidence matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incidence_matrix) for a [D0L](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphic_word#D0L_system) [Lindenmayer system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L-system) on the alphabet ⁠    { a , b , c }   {\displaystyle \{a,b,c\}}  ⁠ with corresponding [substitution rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-Thue_system)       {    a  ↦  b     b  ↦  a c     c  ↦  a         {\displaystyle {\begin{cases}a\;\mapsto \;b\\b\;\mapsto \;ac\\c\;\mapsto \;a\end{cases}}}   and initiator ⁠     w  0   = c   {\displaystyle w_{0}=c}  ⁠. Associated to this string rewriting process is a set composed of three overlapping [self-similar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-similarity) tiles called the [Rauzy fractal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rauzy_fractal), that visualizes the [combinatorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorics_on_words) information contained in a multiple-generation letter sequence.[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-18)

Partitioning the square

# Partitioning the square

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Plastic_ratio&action=edit&section=5)] [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/48/Plastic_square_partitions.svg/500px-Plastic_square_partitions.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Plastic_square_partitions.svg)Three partitions of a square into similar rectangles, 1 = 3·⁠1/3⁠ = ⁠2/3⁠ + 2·⁠1/6⁠ = ⁠1/ρ2 ⁠ + ⁠1/ρ4 ⁠ + ⁠1/ρ8 ⁠.

There are precisely three ways of partitioning a square into three similar rectangles:[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-21)[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-22)

1. The trivial solution given by three congruent rectangles with aspect ratio 3:1.
2. The solution in which two of the three rectangles are congruent and the third one has twice the side lengths of the other two, where the rectangles have aspect ratio 3:2.
3. The solution in which the three rectangles are all of different sizes and where they have aspect ratio *ρ*2. The ratios of the linear sizes of the three rectangles are: *ρ* (large:medium); *ρ*2 (medium:small); and *ρ*3 (large:small). The internal, long edge of the largest rectangle (the square's fault line) divides two of the square's four edges into two segments each that stand to one another in the ratio *ρ.* The internal, coincident short edge of the medium rectangle and long edge of the small rectangle divides one of the square's other, two edges into two segments that stand to one another in the ratio *ρ*4.

The fact that a rectangle of aspect ratio *ρ*2 can be used for dissections of a square into similar rectangles is equivalent to an algebraic property of the number *ρ*2 related to the [Routh–Hurwitz theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Routh%E2%80%93Hurwitz_theorem): all of its conjugates have positive real part.[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-23)[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-24) [![Newton map for the rho\-squared polynomial\.](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4a/RhoSqr_Newton_map.svg/40px-RhoSqr_Newton_map.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:RhoSqr_Newton_map.svg)

# Plastic pentagon

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Plastic_ratio&action=edit&section=7)] [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5f/Plastic_pentagons.svg/330px-Plastic_pentagons.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Plastic_pentagons.svg)A [triskeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triskelion) of plastic pentagons.

A spiral of [equilateral triangles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilateral_triangle) with edges in ratio ⁠    ρ   {\displaystyle \rho }  ⁠ tiles a *plastic [pentagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentagon)* with four angles of 120 and one of 60 degrees.[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-29) The initial triangle is positioned at the left-hand side of a [parallelogram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallelogram) with base to side ratio ⁠    ρ   {\displaystyle \rho }  ⁠ and left base angle 60 degrees, so that two edges of the triangle are collinear with sides of the parallelogram. Scaling the parallelogram in ratio ⁠       1 ρ    ,   {\displaystyle {\tfrac {1}{\rho }},}  ⁠ accompanied with a clockwise rotation by 60 degrees, the horizontal base is mapped onto the third edge of the triangle. The [centre of rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_(mathematics)#Related_definitions_and_terminology) is on the short (falling) diagonal, dividing it in ratio ⁠     ρ  3     {\displaystyle \rho ^{3}}  ⁠, the expansion rate for a half-turn. Iteration of the process traces an infinite, closed sequence of equilateral triangles with pentagonal boundary.

The [logarithmic spiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithmic_spiral) through the vertices of all triangles has polar slope     k =   3 π   ln ⁡ ( ρ ) .   {\displaystyle k={\frac {3}{\pi }}\ln(\rho ).}   For parallelogram base ⁠    ρ   {\displaystyle \rho }  ⁠, the length of the short diagonal is        ρ  2   − ρ + 1     {\displaystyle {\sqrt {\rho ^{2}-\rho +1}}}   with angle     arctan ⁡ (     3   1 − 2 ρ     ) .   {\displaystyle \arctan({\tfrac {\sqrt {3}}{1-2\rho }}).}   The length of the discrete spiral is      ρ  5   =  ∑  n = 0   ∞    ρ  − n   ;   {\displaystyle \rho ^{5}=\sum _{n=0}^{\infty }\rho ^{-n};}   the pentagon has area         3  4     ρ  3   =     3  4     ∑  n = 0   ∞    ρ  − 2 n   .   {\displaystyle {\tfrac {\sqrt {3}}{4}}\rho ^{3}={\tfrac {\sqrt {3}}{4}}\sum _{n=0}^{\infty }\rho ^{-2n}.}

In the vector image, the construction is repeated on each side of a ⁠    ρ   {\displaystyle \rho }  ⁠ triangle. [John Rutherford Boyd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rutherford_Boyd) discovered a related figure, build on the sides of the ⁠     ρ  − 4     {\displaystyle \rho ^{-4}}  ⁠ triangle.[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-30) [![Spiral design by Rutherford Boyd\.](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d6/Plastic_pentagons_alt.svg/40px-Plastic_pentagons_alt.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Plastic_pentagons_alt.svg)

Plastic spiral

# Plastic spiral

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Plastic_ratio&action=edit&section=8)] [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/43/Plastic_spiral.svg/330px-Plastic_spiral.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Plastic_spiral.svg)Two plastic spirals with different initial radii.[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/83/Chambered_nautilus_shell_and_plastic_spiral.svg/330px-Chambered_nautilus_shell_and_plastic_spiral.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Chambered_nautilus_shell_and_plastic_spiral.svg)Chambered nautilus shell and plastic spiral.

A *plastic spiral* is a [logarithmic spiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithmic_spiral) that gets wider by a factor of ⁠    ρ   {\displaystyle \rho }  ⁠ for every quarter turn. It is described by the [polar equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_equation)     r ( θ ) = a exp ⁡ ( k θ ) ,   {\displaystyle r(\theta )=a\exp(k\theta ),}   with initial radius ⁠    a   {\displaystyle a}  ⁠ and parameter     k =   2 π   ln ⁡ ( ρ ) .   {\displaystyle k={\frac {2}{\pi }}\ln(\rho ).}   If drawn on a rectangle with sides in ratio ⁠    ρ   {\displaystyle \rho }  ⁠, the spiral has its pole at the foot of altitude of a triangle on the diagonal and passes through vertices of rectangles with aspect ratio ⁠     ρ  2     {\displaystyle \rho ^{2}}  ⁠ which are perpendicularly aligned and successively scaled by a factor ⁠     ρ  − 1   .   {\displaystyle \rho ^{-1}.}  ⁠

In 1838 [Henry Moseley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Moseley_(mathematician)) noticed that whorls of a shell of the [chambered nautilus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chambered_nautilus) are in geometrical progression: "It will be found that the distance of any two of its whorls measured upon a radius vector is *one-third* that of the next two whorls measured upon the same radius vector ... The curve is therefore a logarithmic spiral."[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-31) Moseley thus gave the expansion rate       3  4    ≈ ρ − 1  /  116   {\displaystyle {\sqrt[{4}]{3}}\approx \rho -1/116}   for a quarter turn.[[e]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-32) Considering the plastic ratio a three-dimensional equivalent of the ubiquitous golden ratio, it appears to be a natural candidate for measuring the shell.[[f]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-34)

History and names

# History and names

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Plastic_ratio&action=edit&section=10)]

ρ was first studied by [Axel Thue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axel_Thue) in 1912 and by [G. H. Hardy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._H._Hardy) in 1919.[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-35) French high school student [Gérard Cordonnier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%A9rard_Cordonnier?action=edit&redlink=1) [[fr](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%A9rard%20Cordonnier)] discovered the ratio for himself in 1924. In his correspon­dence with [Hans van der Laan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_van_der_Laan) a few years later, he called it the radiant number ([French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language): *le nombre radiant*). Van der Laan initially referred to it as the fundamental ratio ([Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_language): *de grondverhouding*), using the plastic number ([Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_language): *het plastische getal*) from the 1950s onward.[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVoet2016note_12-36) In 1944 [Carl Siegel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Ludwig_Siegel) showed that ρ is the smallest possible [Pisot–Vijayaraghavan number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number) and suggested naming it in honour of Thue.

 [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0d/Interieur_bovenkerk%2C_zicht_op_de_middenbeuk_met_koorbanken_voor_de_monniken_-_Mamelis_-_20536587_-_RCE.jpg/330px-Interieur_bovenkerk%2C_zicht_op_de_middenbeuk_met_koorbanken_voor_de_monniken_-_Mamelis_-_20536587_-_RCE.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Interieur_bovenkerk,_zicht_op_de_middenbeuk_met_koorbanken_voor_de_monniken_-_Mamelis_-_20536587_-_RCE.jpg)The 1967 St. Benedictusberg Abbey church designed by Hans van der Laan.

Unlike the names of the [golden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_ratio) and [silver ratios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_ratio), the word plastic was not intended by van der Laan to refer to a specific substance, but rather in its adjectival sense, meaning something that can be given a three-dimensional shape.[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-37) This, according to [Richard Padovan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Padovan), is because the characteristic ratios of the number, ⁠3/4⁠ and ⁠1/7⁠, relate to the limits of human perception in relating one physical size to another. Van der Laan designed the 1967 [St. Benedictusberg Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Benedictusberg_Abbey) church to these plastic number proportions.[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-38)

The plastic number is also sometimes called the silver number, a name given to it by [Midhat J. Gazalé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midhat_J._Gazal%C3%A9) [[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-39) and subsequently used by [Martin Gardner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Gardner),[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-40) but that name is more commonly used for the [silver ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_ratio) 1 + √2, one of the ratios from the family of [metallic means](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metallic_mean) first described by [Vera W. de Spinadel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vera_W._de_Spinadel). Gardner suggested referring to ρ2 as "high phi", and [Donald Knuth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Knuth) created a special typographic mark for this name, a variant of the Greek letter phi ("φ") with its central circle raised, resembling the Georgian letter [pari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pari_(letter)) ("Ⴔ").

Resembling φ (φ−1) = 1, *Mathworld* suggests it is like a "golden ratio for exponentials".[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio#cite_note-33) The interval 31/4 < ρ < Ω−1/2  is smaller than 0.012.
