In [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics), a **Pisot–Vijayaraghavan number** (or **Pisot number** or **PV number**) is a [real](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_number) [algebraic integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_integer) greater than 1, all of whose [Galois conjugates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galois_conjugate) are less than 1 in [absolute value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_value). These numbers were discovered by [Axel Thue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axel_Thue) in 1912 and rediscovered by [G. H. Hardy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._H._Hardy) in 1919 within the context of [Diophantine approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantine_approximation). They became widely known after the publication of [Charles Pisot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Pisot)'s dissertation in 1938. They also occur in the uniqueness problem for [Fourier series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_series). [Tirukkannapuram Vijayaraghavan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tirukkannapuram_Vijayaraghavan) and [Raphael Salem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raphael_Salem) continued their study in the 1940s. [Salem numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salem_number) are a closely related set of numbers.

A characteristic property of PV numbers is that their powers [approach integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almost_integer) at an exponential rate. Pisot [proved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_proof) a remarkable [converse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Converse_(logic)): if *α* > 1 is a real number such that the [sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequence)

     ‖  α  n   ‖   {\displaystyle \|\alpha ^{n}\|}

measuring the distance from its consecutive powers to the nearest [integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer) is [square-summable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequence_space), or *ℓ* 2, then *α* is a Pisot number (and, in particular, algebraic). Building on this characterization of PV numbers, Salem showed that the set *S* of all PV numbers is [closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_set). Its minimal element is a [cubic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_polynomial) irrationality known as the [plastic ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio). Much is known about the [accumulation points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accumulation_point) of *S*. The smallest of them is the [golden ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_ratio).

## Definition and properties

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number&action=edit&section=1)] [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/03/Pisot2_Newton_map.svg/330px-Pisot2_Newton_map.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Pisot2_Newton_map.svg)[Newton's method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_method) for p(z) = z4 − z3 − 1. The second Pisot number χ (right) and its conjugates at the nuclei of their [basins of attraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attractor#Basins_of_attraction) in the complex plane. [Julia set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_set) of the Newton map in orange, with unit circle and real curve for reference.

An **algebraic integer** of degree *n* is a root *α* of an [irreducible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreducible_polynomial) [monic polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monic_polynomial) *P*(*x*) of [degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_of_a_polynomial) *n* with integer coefficients, its **[minimal polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_polynomial_(field_theory))**. The other roots of *P*(*x*) are called the **[conjugates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_element_(field_theory))** of *α*. If *α* > 1 but all other roots of *P*(*x*) are real or [complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_number) numbers of absolute value less than 1, so that they lie strictly inside the [unit circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_circle) in the [complex plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_plane), then *α* is called a **Pisot number**, **Pisot–Vijayaraghavan number**, or simply **PV number**. For example, the [golden ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_ratio), *φ* ≈ 1.618, is a real [quadratic integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_integer) that is greater than 1, while the absolute value of its conjugate, −*φ*−1 ≈ −0.618, is less than 1. Therefore, *φ* is a Pisot number. Its minimal polynomial is *x*2 − *x* − 1.

### Elementary properties

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number&action=edit&section=2)]
- Every integer greater than 1 is a PV number. Conversely, every [rational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_number) PV number is an integer greater than 1.
- If α is an [irrational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrational_number) PV number whose minimal polynomial ends in *k* then α is greater than |*k*|.
- If α is a PV number then so are its powers α*k*, for all positive integer exponents *k*.
- Every real [algebraic number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_number_field) **K** of degree *n* contains a PV number of degree *n*. This number is a field generator. The set of all PV numbers of degree *n* in **K** is closed under multiplication.
- Given an upper bound *M* and degree *n*, there are only [finitely many](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_set) PV numbers of degree *n* that are less than *M*.
- Every PV number is a [Perron number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perron_number) (a real algebraic number greater than one all of whose conjugates have smaller absolute value).

### Diophantine properties

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number&action=edit&section=3)]

The main interest in PV numbers is due to the fact that their powers have a very "biased" distribution (mod 1). If *α* is a PV number and *λ* is any algebraic integer in the [field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_(mathematics))      Q  ( α )   {\displaystyle \mathbb {Q} (\alpha )}   then the sequence

     ‖ λ  α  n   ‖ ,   {\displaystyle \|\lambda \alpha ^{n}\|,}

where ||*x*|| denotes the distance from the real number *x* to the nearest integer, approaches 0 at an exponential rate. In particular, it is a square-summable sequence and its terms converge to 0.

Two converse statements are known: they characterize PV numbers among all real numbers and among the algebraic numbers (but under a weaker Diophantine assumption).

- Suppose *α* is a real number greater than 1 and *λ* is a non-zero real number such that
      ∑  n = 1   ∞   ‖ λ  α  n    ‖  2   < ∞ .   {\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty }\|\lambda \alpha ^{n}\|^{2}<\infty .}   Then *α* is a Pisot number and *λ* is an algebraic number in the field      Q  ( α )   {\displaystyle \mathbb {Q} (\alpha )}   (**Pisot's theorem**).
- Suppose *α* is an algebraic number greater than 1 and *λ* is a non-zero real number such that
     ‖ λ  α  n   ‖ → 0 ,  n → ∞ .   {\displaystyle \|\lambda \alpha ^{n}\|\to 0,\quad n\to \infty .}   Then *α* is a Pisot number and *λ* is an algebraic number in the field      Q  ( α )   {\displaystyle \mathbb {Q} (\alpha )}  .

A longstanding **Pisot–Vijayaraghavan problem** asks whether the assumption that *α* is algebraic can be dropped from the last statement. If the answer is affirmative, Pisot's numbers would be characterized *among all real numbers* by the simple convergence of ||*λα**n*|| to 0 for some auxiliary real *λ*. It is known that there are only [countably many](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable_set) numbers *α* with this property.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number#cite_note-1)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number#cite_note-2) The problem is to decide whether any of them is [transcendental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendental_number).

### Topological properties

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number&action=edit&section=4)]

The set of all Pisot numbers is denoted *S*. Since Pisot numbers are algebraic, the set *S* is countable. Raphael Salem proved that this set is [closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_set): it contains all its [limit points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_point).[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number#cite_note-3) His proof uses a constructive version of the main diophantine property of Pisot numbers:[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number#cite_note-Sal13-4) given a Pisot number *α*, a real number *λ* can be chosen so that 0 < *λ* ≤ *α* and

      ∑  n = 1   ∞   ‖ λ  α  n    ‖  2   ≤ 9.   {\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty }\|\lambda \alpha ^{n}\|^{2}\leq 9.}

Thus the *ℓ* 2 norm of the sequence ||*λα**n*|| can be bounded by a uniform constant independent of *α*. In the last step of the proof, Pisot's characterization is invoked to conclude that the limit of a sequence of Pisot numbers is itself a Pisot number.

Closedness of *S* implies that it has a [minimal element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_element). [Carl Siegel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Ludwig_Siegel) proved that it is the positive root of the equation *x*3 − *x* − 1 = 0 ([plastic constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio)) and is isolated in *S*.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number#cite_note-5) He constructed two sequences of Pisot numbers converging to the golden ratio *φ* from below and asked whether *φ* is the smallest limit point of *S*. This was later proved by Dufresnoy and Pisot, who also determined all elements of *S* that are less than *φ*; not all of them belong to Siegel's two sequences. Vijayaraghavan proved that *S* has infinitely many limit points; in fact, the sequence of [derived sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derived_set_(mathematics))

     S ,  S ′  ,  S ″  , …   {\displaystyle S,S',S'',\ldots }

does not terminate. On the other hand, the intersection      S  ( ω )     {\displaystyle S^{(\omega )}}   of these sets is [empty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empty_set), meaning that the [Cantor–Bendixson rank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derived_set_(mathematics)#Cantor%E2%80%93Bendixson_rank) of *S* is *ω*. Even more accurately, the [order type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_type) of *S* has been determined:[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number#cite_note-6) Let      a  0   = 1   {\displaystyle a_{0}=1}  , and for all *n* ≥ 1, define the order type

      a  n + 1   =  a  n   ω + 1 + (  a  n   ω  )  ∗   ,   {\displaystyle a_{n+1}=a_{n}\omega +1+(a_{n}\omega )^{*},}

where *ω* is the order type of the natural numbers, *a** means *a* in [reverse order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_order), and addition and multiplication are interpreted the same as for [ordinal numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinal_number). Then *S* has order type

      o r d  ( S ) =  ∑  n = 0   ∞    a  n   .   {\displaystyle \mathrm {ord} (S)=\sum _{n=0}^{\infty }a_{n}.}

The set of [Salem numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salem_number), denoted by *T*, is intimately related with *S*. It has been proved that *S* is contained in the set *T'* of the limit points of *T*.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number#cite_note-7)[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number#cite_note-Sal30-8) It has been [conjectured](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjecture) that the [union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_(set_theory)) of *S* and *T* is closed.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number#cite_note-Sal31-9)

## Powers of PV-numbers

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number&action=edit&section=6)]

Pisot–Vijayaraghavan numbers can be used to generate [almost integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almost_integer): the *n*th power of a Pisot number approaches integers as *n* grows. For example,

     ( 3 +   10    )  6   = 27379 + 8658   10   = 54757.9999817 ⋯ ≈ 54758 −   1 54758   .   {\displaystyle (3+{\sqrt {10}})^{6}=27379+8658{\sqrt {10}}=54757.9999817\dots \approx 54758-{\frac {1}{54758}}.}

Since     27379    {\displaystyle 27379\,}   and     8658   10      {\displaystyle 8658{\sqrt {10}}\,}   differ by only     0.0000182 … ,    {\displaystyle 0.0000182\dots ,\,}

       27379 8658   = 3.162277662 …   {\displaystyle {\frac {27379}{8658}}=3.162277662\dots }

is extremely close to

       10   = 3.162277660 … .   {\displaystyle {\sqrt {10}}=3.162277660\dots .}

Indeed

       (   27379 8658   )   2   = 10 +   1  8658  2     .   {\displaystyle \left({\frac {27379}{8658}}\right)^{2}=10+{\frac {1}{8658^{2}}}.}

Higher powers give correspondingly better rational approximations.

This property stems from the fact that for each *n*, the sum of *n*th powers of an algebraic integer *x* and its conjugates is exactly an integer; this follows from an application of [Newton's identities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_identities). When *x* is a Pisot number, the *n*th powers of the other conjugates tend to 0 as *n* tends to infinity. Since the sum is an integer, the distance from *xn* to the nearest integer tends to 0 at an exponential rate.

In [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics), a **Pisot–Vijayaraghavan number** (or **Pisot number** or **PV number**) is a [real](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_number) [algebraic integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_integer) greater than 1, all of whose [Galois conjugates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galois_conjugate) are less than 1 in [absolute value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_value). These numbers were discovered by [Axel Thue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axel_Thue) in 1912 and rediscovered by [G. H. Hardy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._H._Hardy) in 1919 within the context of [Diophantine approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantine_approximation). They became widely known after the publication of [Charles Pisot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Pisot)'s dissertation in 1938. They also occur in the uniqueness problem for [Fourier series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_series). [Tirukkannapuram Vijayaraghavan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tirukkannapuram_Vijayaraghavan) and [Raphael Salem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raphael_Salem) continued their study in the 1940s. [Salem numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salem_number) are a closely related set of numbers.

A characteristic property of PV numbers is that their powers [approach integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almost_integer) at an exponential rate. Pisot [proved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_proof) a remarkable [converse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Converse_(logic)): if *α* > 1 is a real number such that the [sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequence)

     ‖  α  n   ‖   {\displaystyle \|\alpha ^{n}\|}

measuring the distance from its consecutive powers to the nearest [integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer) is [square-summable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequence_space), or *ℓ* 2, then *α* is a Pisot number (and, in particular, algebraic). Building on this characterization of PV numbers, Salem showed that the set *S* of all PV numbers is [closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_set). Its minimal element is a [cubic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_polynomial) irrationality known as the [plastic ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio). Much is known about the [accumulation points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accumulation_point) of *S*. The smallest of them is the [golden ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_ratio).

Definition and properties

# Definition and properties

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number&action=edit&section=1)] [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/03/Pisot2_Newton_map.svg/330px-Pisot2_Newton_map.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Pisot2_Newton_map.svg)[Newton's method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_method) for p(z) = z4 − z3 − 1. The second Pisot number χ (right) and its conjugates at the nuclei of their [basins of attraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attractor#Basins_of_attraction) in the complex plane. [Julia set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_set) of the Newton map in orange, with unit circle and real curve for reference.

An **algebraic integer** of degree *n* is a root *α* of an [irreducible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreducible_polynomial) [monic polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monic_polynomial) *P*(*x*) of [degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_of_a_polynomial) *n* with integer coefficients, its **[minimal polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_polynomial_(field_theory))**. The other roots of *P*(*x*) are called the **[conjugates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_element_(field_theory))** of *α*. If *α* > 1 but all other roots of *P*(*x*) are real or [complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_number) numbers of absolute value less than 1, so that they lie strictly inside the [unit circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_circle) in the [complex plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_plane), then *α* is called a **Pisot number**, **Pisot–Vijayaraghavan number**, or simply **PV number**. For example, the [golden ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_ratio), *φ* ≈ 1.618, is a real [quadratic integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_integer) that is greater than 1, while the absolute value of its conjugate, −*φ*−1 ≈ −0.618, is less than 1. Therefore, *φ* is a Pisot number. Its minimal polynomial is *x*2 − *x* − 1.

Elementary properties

# Elementary properties

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number&action=edit&section=2)]
- Every integer greater than 1 is a PV number. Conversely, every [rational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_number) PV number is an integer greater than 1.
- If α is an [irrational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrational_number) PV number whose minimal polynomial ends in *k* then α is greater than |*k*|.
- If α is a PV number then so are its powers α*k*, for all positive integer exponents *k*.
- Every real [algebraic number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_number_field) **K** of degree *n* contains a PV number of degree *n*. This number is a field generator. The set of all PV numbers of degree *n* in **K** is closed under multiplication.
- Given an upper bound *M* and degree *n*, there are only [finitely many](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_set) PV numbers of degree *n* that are less than *M*.
- Every PV number is a [Perron number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perron_number) (a real algebraic number greater than one all of whose conjugates have smaller absolute value).

Diophantine properties

# Diophantine properties

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number&action=edit&section=3)]

The main interest in PV numbers is due to the fact that their powers have a very "biased" distribution (mod 1). If *α* is a PV number and *λ* is any algebraic integer in the [field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_(mathematics))      Q  ( α )   {\displaystyle \mathbb {Q} (\alpha )}   then the sequence

     ‖ λ  α  n   ‖ ,   {\displaystyle \|\lambda \alpha ^{n}\|,}

where ||*x*|| denotes the distance from the real number *x* to the nearest integer, approaches 0 at an exponential rate. In particular, it is a square-summable sequence and its terms converge to 0.

Two converse statements are known: they characterize PV numbers among all real numbers and among the algebraic numbers (but under a weaker Diophantine assumption).

- Suppose *α* is a real number greater than 1 and *λ* is a non-zero real number such that
      ∑  n = 1   ∞   ‖ λ  α  n    ‖  2   < ∞ .   {\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty }\|\lambda \alpha ^{n}\|^{2}<\infty .}   Then *α* is a Pisot number and *λ* is an algebraic number in the field      Q  ( α )   {\displaystyle \mathbb {Q} (\alpha )}   (**Pisot's theorem**).
- Suppose *α* is an algebraic number greater than 1 and *λ* is a non-zero real number such that
     ‖ λ  α  n   ‖ → 0 ,  n → ∞ .   {\displaystyle \|\lambda \alpha ^{n}\|\to 0,\quad n\to \infty .}   Then *α* is a Pisot number and *λ* is an algebraic number in the field      Q  ( α )   {\displaystyle \mathbb {Q} (\alpha )}  .

A longstanding **Pisot–Vijayaraghavan problem** asks whether the assumption that *α* is algebraic can be dropped from the last statement. If the answer is affirmative, Pisot's numbers would be characterized *among all real numbers* by the simple convergence of ||*λα**n*|| to 0 for some auxiliary real *λ*. It is known that there are only [countably many](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable_set) numbers *α* with this property.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number#cite_note-1)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number#cite_note-2) The problem is to decide whether any of them is [transcendental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendental_number).

Topological properties

# Topological properties

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number&action=edit&section=4)]

The set of all Pisot numbers is denoted *S*. Since Pisot numbers are algebraic, the set *S* is countable. Raphael Salem proved that this set is [closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_set): it contains all its [limit points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_point).[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number#cite_note-3) His proof uses a constructive version of the main diophantine property of Pisot numbers:[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number#cite_note-Sal13-4) given a Pisot number *α*, a real number *λ* can be chosen so that 0 < *λ* ≤ *α* and

      ∑  n = 1   ∞   ‖ λ  α  n    ‖  2   ≤ 9.   {\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty }\|\lambda \alpha ^{n}\|^{2}\leq 9.}

Thus the *ℓ* 2 norm of the sequence ||*λα**n*|| can be bounded by a uniform constant independent of *α*. In the last step of the proof, Pisot's characterization is invoked to conclude that the limit of a sequence of Pisot numbers is itself a Pisot number.

Closedness of *S* implies that it has a [minimal element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_element). [Carl Siegel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Ludwig_Siegel) proved that it is the positive root of the equation *x*3 − *x* − 1 = 0 ([plastic constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_ratio)) and is isolated in *S*.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number#cite_note-5) He constructed two sequences of Pisot numbers converging to the golden ratio *φ* from below and asked whether *φ* is the smallest limit point of *S*. This was later proved by Dufresnoy and Pisot, who also determined all elements of *S* that are less than *φ*; not all of them belong to Siegel's two sequences. Vijayaraghavan proved that *S* has infinitely many limit points; in fact, the sequence of [derived sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derived_set_(mathematics))

     S ,  S ′  ,  S ″  , …   {\displaystyle S,S',S'',\ldots }

does not terminate. On the other hand, the intersection      S  ( ω )     {\displaystyle S^{(\omega )}}   of these sets is [empty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empty_set), meaning that the [Cantor–Bendixson rank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derived_set_(mathematics)#Cantor%E2%80%93Bendixson_rank) of *S* is *ω*. Even more accurately, the [order type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_type) of *S* has been determined:[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number#cite_note-6) Let      a  0   = 1   {\displaystyle a_{0}=1}  , and for all *n* ≥ 1, define the order type

      a  n + 1   =  a  n   ω + 1 + (  a  n   ω  )  ∗   ,   {\displaystyle a_{n+1}=a_{n}\omega +1+(a_{n}\omega )^{*},}

where *ω* is the order type of the natural numbers, *a** means *a* in [reverse order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_order), and addition and multiplication are interpreted the same as for [ordinal numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinal_number). Then *S* has order type

      o r d  ( S ) =  ∑  n = 0   ∞    a  n   .   {\displaystyle \mathrm {ord} (S)=\sum _{n=0}^{\infty }a_{n}.}

The set of [Salem numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salem_number), denoted by *T*, is intimately related with *S*. It has been proved that *S* is contained in the set *T'* of the limit points of *T*.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number#cite_note-7)[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number#cite_note-Sal30-8) It has been [conjectured](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjecture) that the [union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_(set_theory)) of *S* and *T* is closed.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number#cite_note-Sal31-9)

Powers of PV-numbers

# Powers of PV-numbers

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number&action=edit&section=6)]

Pisot–Vijayaraghavan numbers can be used to generate [almost integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almost_integer): the *n*th power of a Pisot number approaches integers as *n* grows. For example,

     ( 3 +   10    )  6   = 27379 + 8658   10   = 54757.9999817 ⋯ ≈ 54758 −   1 54758   .   {\displaystyle (3+{\sqrt {10}})^{6}=27379+8658{\sqrt {10}}=54757.9999817\dots \approx 54758-{\frac {1}{54758}}.}

Since     27379    {\displaystyle 27379\,}   and     8658   10      {\displaystyle 8658{\sqrt {10}}\,}   differ by only     0.0000182 … ,    {\displaystyle 0.0000182\dots ,\,}

       27379 8658   = 3.162277662 …   {\displaystyle {\frac {27379}{8658}}=3.162277662\dots }

is extremely close to

       10   = 3.162277660 … .   {\displaystyle {\sqrt {10}}=3.162277660\dots .}

Indeed

       (   27379 8658   )   2   = 10 +   1  8658  2     .   {\displaystyle \left({\frac {27379}{8658}}\right)^{2}=10+{\frac {1}{8658^{2}}}.}

Higher powers give correspondingly better rational approximations.

This property stems from the fact that for each *n*, the sum of *n*th powers of an algebraic integer *x* and its conjugates is exactly an integer; this follows from an application of [Newton's identities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_identities). When *x* is a Pisot number, the *n*th powers of the other conjugates tend to 0 as *n* tends to infinity. Since the sum is an integer, the distance from *xn* to the nearest integer tends to 0 at an exponential rate.

Since these PV numbers are less than 2, they are all units: their minimal polynomials end in 1 or −1.  The polynomials in this table,[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number#cite_note-:0-11) with the exception of

      x  6   − 2  x  5   +  x  4   −  x  2   + x − 1 ,   {\displaystyle x^{6}-2x^{5}+x^{4}-x^{2}+x-1,}

are factors of either

      x  n   (  x  2   − x − 1 ) + 1   {\displaystyle x^{n}(x^{2}-x-1)+1}

or

      x  n   (  x  2   − x − 1 ) + (  x  2   − 1 ) .   {\displaystyle x^{n}(x^{2}-x-1)+(x^{2}-1).}

The first polynomial is divisible by *x*2 − 1 when *n* is odd and by *x* − 1 when *n* is [even](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parity_(mathematics)). It has one other real zero, which is a PV number. Dividing either polynomial by *x**n* gives expressions that approach *x*2 − *x* − 1 as *n* grows very large and have zeros that [converge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_of_a_sequence) to *φ*. A complementary pair of polynomials,

      x  n   (  x  2   − x − 1 ) − 1   {\displaystyle x^{n}(x^{2}-x-1)-1}

and

      x  n   (  x  2   − x − 1 ) − (  x  2   − 1 )    {\displaystyle x^{n}(x^{2}-x-1)-(x^{2}-1)\,}

yields Pisot numbers that approach φ from above.

Two-dimensional [turbulence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbulence) modeling using [logarithmic spiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithmic_spiral) chains with [self-similarity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-similarity) defined by a constant scaling factor can be reproduced with some small Pisot numbers.[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisot%E2%80%93Vijayaraghavan_number#cite_note-12)
