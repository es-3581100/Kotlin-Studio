# Lattice reduction

- [Українська](https://uk.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D0%97%D0%B2%D0%B5%D0%B4%D0%B5%D0%BD%D0%BD%D1%8F_%D2%91%D1%80%D0%B0%D1%82%D0%BA%D0%B8)
- [中文](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%A0%BC%E8%A7%84%E7%BA%A6)
 [Edit links](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Special:EntityPage/Q6497132#sitelinks-wikipedia)                          From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia   Mathematical operation [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/27/Lattice-reduction.svg/330px-Lattice-reduction.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Lattice-reduction.svg)Lattice reduction in two dimensions: the black vectors are the given basis for the lattice (represented by blue dots), the red vectors are the reduced basis

In mathematics, the goal of **lattice basis reduction** is to find a [basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_(linear_algebra)) with short, nearly [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal) vectors when given an integer [lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_(group)) basis as input. This is realized using different algorithms, whose running time is usually at least exponential in the dimension of the lattice.

Finding a reduced lattice basis is also closely related to the problem in crystallography of finding a unique unit cell. Historically reduction theory was first studied by Lagrange (1773) and independently by Gauss (1801), in order to classify [binary quadratic forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_quadratic_form), a classic problem in number theory. In a little sidenote to a book review in 1831, Gauss mentions that the reduction theory for certain quadratic forms is equivalent to finding a unit cell for point lattices and he sees the relevance for crystallography. This close relationship in number theory and geometry of point lattices inspired much of the successive work on quadratic forms culminating in [Minkowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski)'s important work "Geometry of Numbers" (1896 and 1910).

## Nearly orthogonal

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Lattice_reduction&action=edit&section=1)]

One measure of *nearly orthogonal* is the **orthogonality defect**. This compares the product of the lengths of the basis vectors with the volume of the [parallelepiped](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallelepiped) they define. For perfectly orthogonal basis vectors, these quantities would be the same.

Any particular basis of     n   {\displaystyle n}   vectors may be represented by a [matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_(mathematics))     B   {\displaystyle B}  , whose columns are the basis vectors      b  i   , i = 1 , … , n   {\displaystyle b_{i},i=1,\ldots ,n}  . In the **fully dimensional** case where the number of basis vectors is equal to the dimension of the space they occupy, this matrix is square, and the volume of the fundamental parallelepiped is simply the absolute value of the [determinant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Determinant) of this matrix     det ( B )   {\displaystyle \det(B)}  . If the number of vectors is less than the dimension of the underlying space, then volume is       det (  B  T   B )     {\displaystyle {\sqrt {\det(B^{T}B)}}}  . For a given lattice     Λ   {\displaystyle \Lambda }  , this volume is the same (up to sign) for any basis, and hence is referred to as the determinant of the lattice     det ( Λ )   {\displaystyle \det(\Lambda )}   or **lattice constant**     d ( Λ )   {\displaystyle d(\Lambda )}  .

The orthogonality defect is the product of the basis vector lengths divided by the parallelepiped volume;

     δ ( B ) =     Π  i = 1   n   ‖  b  i   ‖   det (  B  T   B )    =     Π  i = 1   n   ‖  b  i   ‖   d ( Λ )      {\displaystyle \delta (B)={\frac {\Pi _{i=1}^{n}\|b_{i}\|}{\sqrt {\det(B^{T}B)}}}={\frac {\Pi _{i=1}^{n}\|b_{i}\|}{d(\Lambda )}}}

From the geometric definition it may be appreciated that     δ ( B ) ≥ 1   {\displaystyle \delta (B)\geq 1}   with equality if and only if the basis is orthogonal.

If the lattice reduction problem is defined as finding the basis with the smallest possible defect, then the problem is [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete).[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_reduction#cite_note-Yap-1) However, there exist [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time) algorithms to find a basis with defect     δ ( B ) ≤ c   {\displaystyle \delta (B)\leq c}   where *c* is some constant depending only on the number of basis vectors and the dimension of the underlying space (if different).[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_reduction#cite_note-LLL_1982-2)[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_reduction#cite_note-Yap-1) This is a good enough solution in many practical applications, such as in the factoring of polynomials[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_reduction#cite_note-LLL_1982-2)

## In two dimensions

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Lattice_reduction&action=edit&section=2)]

## Applications

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Lattice_reduction&action=edit&section=3)]

Lattice reduction algorithms are used in a number of modern number theoretical applications, including in the discovery of a [spigot algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spigot_algorithm) for     π   {\displaystyle \pi }  . Although determining the shortest basis is possibly an NP-complete problem, algorithms such as the [LLL algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenstra%E2%80%93Lenstra%E2%80%93Lov%C3%A1sz_lattice_basis_reduction_algorithm)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_reduction#cite_note-LLL_1982-2) can find a short (not necessarily shortest) basis in polynomial time with guaranteed worst-case performance. [LLL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenstra%E2%80%93Lenstra%E2%80%93Lov%C3%A1sz_lattice_basis_reduction_algorithm) is widely used in the [cryptanalysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptanalysis) of [public key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public-key_cryptography) cryptosystems.

When used to find integer relations, a typical input to the algorithm consists of an augmented     n × n   {\displaystyle n\times n}   identity matrix with the entries in the last column consisting of the     n   {\displaystyle n}   elements (multiplied by a large positive constant     w   {\displaystyle w}   to penalize vectors that do not sum to zero) between which the relation is sought.

The [LLL algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LLL_algorithm) for computing a nearly-orthogonal basis was used to show that [integer programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer_programming) in any fixed dimension can be done in [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P_(complexity)).[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_reduction#cite_note-Lenstra_1983-4)

Lattice reduction

# Lattice reduction

- [Українська](https://uk.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D0%97%D0%B2%D0%B5%D0%B4%D0%B5%D0%BD%D0%BD%D1%8F_%D2%91%D1%80%D0%B0%D1%82%D0%BA%D0%B8)
- [中文](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%A0%BC%E8%A7%84%E7%BA%A6)
 [Edit links](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Special:EntityPage/Q6497132#sitelinks-wikipedia)                          From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia   Mathematical operation [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/27/Lattice-reduction.svg/330px-Lattice-reduction.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Lattice-reduction.svg)Lattice reduction in two dimensions: the black vectors are the given basis for the lattice (represented by blue dots), the red vectors are the reduced basis

In mathematics, the goal of **lattice basis reduction** is to find a [basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_(linear_algebra)) with short, nearly [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal) vectors when given an integer [lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_(group)) basis as input. This is realized using different algorithms, whose running time is usually at least exponential in the dimension of the lattice.

Finding a reduced lattice basis is also closely related to the problem in crystallography of finding a unique unit cell. Historically reduction theory was first studied by Lagrange (1773) and independently by Gauss (1801), in order to classify [binary quadratic forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_quadratic_form), a classic problem in number theory. In a little sidenote to a book review in 1831, Gauss mentions that the reduction theory for certain quadratic forms is equivalent to finding a unit cell for point lattices and he sees the relevance for crystallography. This close relationship in number theory and geometry of point lattices inspired much of the successive work on quadratic forms culminating in [Minkowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski)'s important work "Geometry of Numbers" (1896 and 1910).

Nearly orthogonal

# Nearly orthogonal

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Lattice_reduction&action=edit&section=1)]

One measure of *nearly orthogonal* is the **orthogonality defect**. This compares the product of the lengths of the basis vectors with the volume of the [parallelepiped](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallelepiped) they define. For perfectly orthogonal basis vectors, these quantities would be the same.

Any particular basis of     n   {\displaystyle n}   vectors may be represented by a [matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_(mathematics))     B   {\displaystyle B}  , whose columns are the basis vectors      b  i   , i = 1 , … , n   {\displaystyle b_{i},i=1,\ldots ,n}  . In the **fully dimensional** case where the number of basis vectors is equal to the dimension of the space they occupy, this matrix is square, and the volume of the fundamental parallelepiped is simply the absolute value of the [determinant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Determinant) of this matrix     det ( B )   {\displaystyle \det(B)}  . If the number of vectors is less than the dimension of the underlying space, then volume is       det (  B  T   B )     {\displaystyle {\sqrt {\det(B^{T}B)}}}  . For a given lattice     Λ   {\displaystyle \Lambda }  , this volume is the same (up to sign) for any basis, and hence is referred to as the determinant of the lattice     det ( Λ )   {\displaystyle \det(\Lambda )}   or **lattice constant**     d ( Λ )   {\displaystyle d(\Lambda )}  .

The orthogonality defect is the product of the basis vector lengths divided by the parallelepiped volume;

     δ ( B ) =     Π  i = 1   n   ‖  b  i   ‖   det (  B  T   B )    =     Π  i = 1   n   ‖  b  i   ‖   d ( Λ )      {\displaystyle \delta (B)={\frac {\Pi _{i=1}^{n}\|b_{i}\|}{\sqrt {\det(B^{T}B)}}}={\frac {\Pi _{i=1}^{n}\|b_{i}\|}{d(\Lambda )}}}

From the geometric definition it may be appreciated that     δ ( B ) ≥ 1   {\displaystyle \delta (B)\geq 1}   with equality if and only if the basis is orthogonal.

If the lattice reduction problem is defined as finding the basis with the smallest possible defect, then the problem is [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete).[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_reduction#cite_note-Yap-1) However, there exist [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time) algorithms to find a basis with defect     δ ( B ) ≤ c   {\displaystyle \delta (B)\leq c}   where *c* is some constant depending only on the number of basis vectors and the dimension of the underlying space (if different).[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_reduction#cite_note-LLL_1982-2)[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_reduction#cite_note-Yap-1) This is a good enough solution in many practical applications, such as in the factoring of polynomials[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_reduction#cite_note-LLL_1982-2)

Applications

# Applications

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Lattice_reduction&action=edit&section=3)]

Lattice reduction algorithms are used in a number of modern number theoretical applications, including in the discovery of a [spigot algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spigot_algorithm) for     π   {\displaystyle \pi }  . Although determining the shortest basis is possibly an NP-complete problem, algorithms such as the [LLL algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenstra%E2%80%93Lenstra%E2%80%93Lov%C3%A1sz_lattice_basis_reduction_algorithm)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_reduction#cite_note-LLL_1982-2) can find a short (not necessarily shortest) basis in polynomial time with guaranteed worst-case performance. [LLL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenstra%E2%80%93Lenstra%E2%80%93Lov%C3%A1sz_lattice_basis_reduction_algorithm) is widely used in the [cryptanalysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptanalysis) of [public key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public-key_cryptography) cryptosystems.

When used to find integer relations, a typical input to the algorithm consists of an augmented     n × n   {\displaystyle n\times n}   identity matrix with the entries in the last column consisting of the     n   {\displaystyle n}   elements (multiplied by a large positive constant     w   {\displaystyle w}   to penalize vectors that do not sum to zero) between which the relation is sought.

The [LLL algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LLL_algorithm) for computing a nearly-orthogonal basis was used to show that [integer programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer_programming) in any fixed dimension can be done in [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P_(complexity)).[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_reduction#cite_note-Lenstra_1983-4)
