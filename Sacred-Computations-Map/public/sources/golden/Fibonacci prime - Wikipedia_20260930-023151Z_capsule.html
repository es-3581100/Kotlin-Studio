A **Fibonacci prime** is a [Fibonacci number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_number) that is [prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number), a type of integer sequence prime.

The first Fibonacci primes are (sequence [A005478](https://oeis.org/A005478) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences)):

## Known Fibonacci primes

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fibonacci_prime&action=edit&section=1)]  Unsolved problem in mathematics Are there an infinite number of Fibonacci primes? [More unsolved problems in mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_unsolved_problems_in_mathematics)

It is not known whether there are [infinitely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinity) many Fibonacci primes. With the indexing starting with *F*1 = *F*2 = 1, the first 37 indices *n* for which *F**n* is prime are (sequence [A001605](https://oeis.org/A001605) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences)):

 *n* = 3, 4, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 23, 29, 43, 47, 83, 131, 137, 359, 431, 433, 449, 509, 569, 571, 2971, 4723, 5387, 9311, 9677, 14431, 25561, 30757, 35999, 37511, 50833, 81839, 104911, 130021, 148091, 201107.

(The values *F**n* rapidly become very large so only the indices, *n*, are listed.)

In addition to these proven Fibonacci primes, several [probable primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probable_prime) have been found:

 *n* = 397379, 433781, 590041, 593689, 604711, 931517, 1049897, 1285607, 1636007, 1803059, 1968721, 2904353, 3244369, 3340367, 4740217, 6530879, 7789819, 10317107, 10367321, 11964299.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_prime#cite_note-prptop-2)

Except for the case *n* = 4, all Fibonacci primes have a prime index, because if *a* [divides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisor) *b*, then      F  a     {\displaystyle F_{a}}   also divides      F  b     {\displaystyle F_{b}}   (but not every prime index results in a Fibonacci prime). That is to say, the Fibonacci sequence is a [divisibility sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisibility_sequence).

*F**p* is prime for 8 of the first 10 primes *p*; the exceptions are *F*2 = 1 and *F*19 = 4181 = 37 × 113. However, Fibonacci primes appear to become rarer as the index increases. *F**p* is prime for only 26 of the 1229 primes *p* smaller than 10,000.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_prime#cite_note-3) The number of prime factors in the Fibonacci numbers with prime index are:

 0, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 2, 1, 1, 2, 3, 2, 1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 3, 2, 2, 2, 1, 2, 4, 2, 3, 2, 2, 2, 2, 1, 1, 3, 4, 2, 4, 4, 2, 2, 3, 3, 2, 2, 4, 2, 4, 4, 2, 5, 3, 4, 3, 2, 3, 3, 4, 2, 2, 3, 4, 2, 4, 4, 4, 3, 2, 3, 5, 4, 2, 1, ... (sequence [A080345](https://oeis.org/A080345) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences))

As of September 2023[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fibonacci_prime&action=edit), the largest known certain Fibonacci prime is *F*201107, with 42029 digits. It was proved prime by Maia Karpovich in September 2023.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_prime#cite_note-4)[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_prime#cite_note-5) The largest known probable Fibonacci prime is *F*11964299. It was found by Ryan Propper in June 2025.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_prime#cite_note-prptop-2) It was proved by Nick MacKinnon that the only Fibonacci numbers that are also [twin primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_prime) are 3, 5, and 13.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_prime#cite_note-6)

## Divisibility of Fibonacci numbers

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fibonacci_prime&action=edit&section=2)]

A prime     p   {\displaystyle p}   divides      F  p − 1     {\displaystyle F_{p-1}}   [if and only if](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/If_and_only_if) *p* is [congruent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_arithmetic) to ±1 modulo 5, and *p* divides      F  p + 1     {\displaystyle F_{p+1}}   if and only if it is congruent to ±2 modulo 5. (For *p* = 5, *F*5 = 5 so 5 divides *F*5)

Fibonacci numbers that have a prime index *p* do not share any common divisors greater than 1 with the preceding Fibonacci numbers, due to the identity:[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_prime#cite_note-7)

     gcd (  F  n   ,  F  m   ) =  F  gcd ( n , m )   .   {\displaystyle \gcd(F_{n},F_{m})=F_{\gcd(n,m)}.}

For *n* ≥ 3, *Fn* divides *Fm* if and only if *n* divides *m*.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_prime#cite_note-8)

If we suppose that *m* is a prime number *p*, and *n* is less than *p*, then it is clear that *F**p* cannot share any common divisors with the preceding Fibonacci numbers.

     gcd (  F  p   ,  F  n   ) =  F  gcd ( p , n )   =  F  1   = 1.   {\displaystyle \gcd(F_{p},F_{n})=F_{\gcd(p,n)}=F_{1}=1.}

This means that *Fp* will always have characteristic factors or be a prime characteristic factor itself. The number of distinct prime factors of each Fibonacci number can be put into simple terms.

- *F**nk* is a multiple of *F**k* for all values of *n* and *k* such that *n* ≥ 1 and *k* ≥ 1.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_prime#cite_note-9) It's safe to say that *Fnk* will have "at least" the same number of distinct prime factors as *Fk*. All *F**p* will have no factors of *F**k*, but "at least" one new characteristic prime from [Carmichael's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carmichael's_theorem).
- Carmichael's Theorem applies to all Fibonacci numbers except four special cases:      F  1   =  F  2   = 1 ,  F  6   = 8   {\displaystyle F_{1}=F_{2}=1,F_{6}=8}   and      F  12   = 144.   {\displaystyle F_{12}=144.}   If we look at the prime factors of a Fibonacci number, there will be at least one of them that has never before appeared as a factor in any earlier Fibonacci number. ## Rank of apparition

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fibonacci_prime&action=edit&section=3)]

For a prime *p*, the smallest index *u* > 0 such that *Fu* is divisible by *p* is called the **rank of apparition** (sometimes called **Fibonacci entry point**) of *p* and denoted *a*(*p*). The rank of apparition *a*(*p*) is defined for every prime *p*.[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_prime#cite_note-11) The rank of apparition divides the [Pisano period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisano_period) π(*p*) and allows to determine all Fibonacci numbers divisible by *p*.[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_prime#cite_note-12)

For the divisibility of Fibonacci numbers by powers of a prime,     p ⩾ 3 , n ⩾ 2   {\displaystyle p\geqslant 3,n\geqslant 2}   and     k ⩾ 0   {\displaystyle k\geqslant 0}

      p  n   ∣  F  a ( p ) k  p  n − 1     .   {\displaystyle p^{n}\mid F_{a(p)kp^{n-1}}.}

In particular

      p  2   ∣  F  a ( p ) p   .   {\displaystyle p^{2}\mid F_{a(p)p}.}

## Fibonacci numbers in prime-like sequences

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fibonacci_prime&action=edit&section=6)]

Although it is not known whether there are infinitely many Fibonacci numbers which are prime, [Melfi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giuseppe_Melfi) proved that there are infinitely many which are [practical numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Practical_number),[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_prime#cite_note-15) a sequence which resembles the primes in some respects.

Known Fibonacci primes

# Known Fibonacci primes

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fibonacci_prime&action=edit&section=1)]  Unsolved problem in mathematics Are there an infinite number of Fibonacci primes? [More unsolved problems in mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_unsolved_problems_in_mathematics)

It is not known whether there are [infinitely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinity) many Fibonacci primes. With the indexing starting with *F*1 = *F*2 = 1, the first 37 indices *n* for which *F**n* is prime are (sequence [A001605](https://oeis.org/A001605) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences)):

 *n* = 3, 4, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 23, 29, 43, 47, 83, 131, 137, 359, 431, 433, 449, 509, 569, 571, 2971, 4723, 5387, 9311, 9677, 14431, 25561, 30757, 35999, 37511, 50833, 81839, 104911, 130021, 148091, 201107.

(The values *F**n* rapidly become very large so only the indices, *n*, are listed.)

In addition to these proven Fibonacci primes, several [probable primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probable_prime) have been found:

 *n* = 397379, 433781, 590041, 593689, 604711, 931517, 1049897, 1285607, 1636007, 1803059, 1968721, 2904353, 3244369, 3340367, 4740217, 6530879, 7789819, 10317107, 10367321, 11964299.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_prime#cite_note-prptop-2)

Except for the case *n* = 4, all Fibonacci primes have a prime index, because if *a* [divides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisor) *b*, then      F  a     {\displaystyle F_{a}}   also divides      F  b     {\displaystyle F_{b}}   (but not every prime index results in a Fibonacci prime). That is to say, the Fibonacci sequence is a [divisibility sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisibility_sequence).

*F**p* is prime for 8 of the first 10 primes *p*; the exceptions are *F*2 = 1 and *F*19 = 4181 = 37 × 113. However, Fibonacci primes appear to become rarer as the index increases. *F**p* is prime for only 26 of the 1229 primes *p* smaller than 10,000.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_prime#cite_note-3) The number of prime factors in the Fibonacci numbers with prime index are:

 0, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 2, 1, 1, 2, 3, 2, 1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 3, 2, 2, 2, 1, 2, 4, 2, 3, 2, 2, 2, 2, 1, 1, 3, 4, 2, 4, 4, 2, 2, 3, 3, 2, 2, 4, 2, 4, 4, 2, 5, 3, 4, 3, 2, 3, 3, 4, 2, 2, 3, 4, 2, 4, 4, 4, 3, 2, 3, 5, 4, 2, 1, ... (sequence [A080345](https://oeis.org/A080345) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences))

As of September 2023[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fibonacci_prime&action=edit), the largest known certain Fibonacci prime is *F*201107, with 42029 digits. It was proved prime by Maia Karpovich in September 2023.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_prime#cite_note-4)[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_prime#cite_note-5) The largest known probable Fibonacci prime is *F*11964299. It was found by Ryan Propper in June 2025.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_prime#cite_note-prptop-2) It was proved by Nick MacKinnon that the only Fibonacci numbers that are also [twin primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_prime) are 3, 5, and 13.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_prime#cite_note-6)

Divisibility of Fibonacci numbers

# Divisibility of Fibonacci numbers

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fibonacci_prime&action=edit&section=2)]

A prime     p   {\displaystyle p}   divides      F  p − 1     {\displaystyle F_{p-1}}   [if and only if](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/If_and_only_if) *p* is [congruent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_arithmetic) to ±1 modulo 5, and *p* divides      F  p + 1     {\displaystyle F_{p+1}}   if and only if it is congruent to ±2 modulo 5. (For *p* = 5, *F*5 = 5 so 5 divides *F*5)

Fibonacci numbers that have a prime index *p* do not share any common divisors greater than 1 with the preceding Fibonacci numbers, due to the identity:[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_prime#cite_note-7)

     gcd (  F  n   ,  F  m   ) =  F  gcd ( n , m )   .   {\displaystyle \gcd(F_{n},F_{m})=F_{\gcd(n,m)}.}

For *n* ≥ 3, *Fn* divides *Fm* if and only if *n* divides *m*.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_prime#cite_note-8)

If we suppose that *m* is a prime number *p*, and *n* is less than *p*, then it is clear that *F**p* cannot share any common divisors with the preceding Fibonacci numbers.

     gcd (  F  p   ,  F  n   ) =  F  gcd ( p , n )   =  F  1   = 1.   {\displaystyle \gcd(F_{p},F_{n})=F_{\gcd(p,n)}=F_{1}=1.}

This means that *Fp* will always have characteristic factors or be a prime characteristic factor itself. The number of distinct prime factors of each Fibonacci number can be put into simple terms.

- *F**nk* is a multiple of *F**k* for all values of *n* and *k* such that *n* ≥ 1 and *k* ≥ 1.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_prime#cite_note-9) It's safe to say that *Fnk* will have "at least" the same number of distinct prime factors as *Fk*. All *F**p* will have no factors of *F**k*, but "at least" one new characteristic prime from [Carmichael's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carmichael's_theorem).
- Carmichael's Theorem applies to all Fibonacci numbers except four special cases:      F  1   =  F  2   = 1 ,  F  6   = 8   {\displaystyle F_{1}=F_{2}=1,F_{6}=8}   and      F  12   = 144.   {\displaystyle F_{12}=144.}   If we look at the prime factors of a Fibonacci number, there will be at least one of them that has never before appeared as a factor in any earlier Fibonacci number. Let *πn* be the number of distinct prime factors of *Fn*. (sequence [A022307](https://oeis.org/A022307) in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-Line_Encyclopedia_of_Integer_Sequences))
 If *k* | *n* then      π  n   ⩾  π  k   + 1   {\displaystyle \pi _{n}\geqslant \pi _{k}+1}   except for      π  6   =  π  3   = 1.   {\displaystyle \pi _{6}=\pi _{3}=1.}   If *k* = 1, and *n* is an [odd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parity_(mathematics)) prime, then 1 | *p* and      π  p   ⩾  π  1   + 1 = 1.   {\displaystyle \pi _{p}\geqslant \pi _{1}+1=1.}     *n* 01**2****3**4**5**6**7**8910**11**12**13**141516**17**18**19**202122**23**2425  *Fn* 0112358132134558914423337761098715972584418167651094617711286574636875025  *πn* 00011111222121233132432142

The first step in finding the characteristic quotient of any *Fn* is to divide out the prime factors of all earlier Fibonacci numbers *Fk* for which *k* | *n*.[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_prime#cite_note-10)

The exact quotients left over are prime factors that have not yet appeared.

If *p* and *q* are both primes, then all factors of *Fpq* are characteristic, except for those of *Fp* and *Fq*.

Therefore:

Rank of apparition

# Rank of apparition

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fibonacci_prime&action=edit&section=3)]

For a prime *p*, the smallest index *u* > 0 such that *Fu* is divisible by *p* is called the **rank of apparition** (sometimes called **Fibonacci entry point**) of *p* and denoted *a*(*p*). The rank of apparition *a*(*p*) is defined for every prime *p*.[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_prime#cite_note-11) The rank of apparition divides the [Pisano period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisano_period) π(*p*) and allows to determine all Fibonacci numbers divisible by *p*.[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_prime#cite_note-12)

For the divisibility of Fibonacci numbers by powers of a prime,     p ⩾ 3 , n ⩾ 2   {\displaystyle p\geqslant 3,n\geqslant 2}   and     k ⩾ 0   {\displaystyle k\geqslant 0}

      p  n   ∣  F  a ( p ) k  p  n − 1     .   {\displaystyle p^{n}\mid F_{a(p)kp^{n-1}}.}

In particular

      p  2   ∣  F  a ( p ) p   .   {\displaystyle p^{2}\mid F_{a(p)p}.}

      (   p 5   )  =   {    1   p ≡ ± 1  mod  5       − 1   p ≡ ± 2  mod  5           {\displaystyle \left({\frac {p}{5}}\right)={\begin{cases}1&p\equiv \pm 1{\bmod {5}}\\-1&p\equiv \pm 2{\bmod {5}}\end{cases}}}

Fibonacci primitive part

# Fibonacci primitive part

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fibonacci_prime&action=edit&section=5)]

Because      F  a    |   F  a b     {\displaystyle F_{a}|F_{ab}}  , we can divide any Fibonacci number      F  n     {\displaystyle F_{n}}   by the [least common multiple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_common_multiple) of all      F  d     {\displaystyle F_{d}}   where     d  |  n   {\displaystyle d|n}  . The result is called the **primitive part** of      F  n     {\displaystyle F_{n}}  . The primitive parts of the Fibonacci numbers are

 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 4, 13, 7, 17, 11, 89, 6, 233, 29, 61, 47, 1597, 19, 4181, 41, 421, 199, 28657, 46, 15005, 521, 5777, 281, 514229, 31, 1346269, 2207, 19801, 3571, 141961, 321, 24157817, 9349, 135721, 2161, 165580141, 211, 433494437, 13201, 109441, ... Fibonacci numbers in prime-like sequences

# Fibonacci numbers in prime-like sequences

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fibonacci_prime&action=edit&section=6)]

Although it is not known whether there are infinitely many Fibonacci numbers which are prime, [Melfi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giuseppe_Melfi) proved that there are infinitely many which are [practical numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Practical_number),[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_prime#cite_note-15) a sequence which resembles the primes in some respects.
