In many areas of science, **Bragg's law** – also known as **[Wulff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Wulff)–Bragg's condition** or **[Laue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_von_Laue)–Bragg interference** – is a special case of [Laue diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laue_diffraction) that gives the angles for coherent [scattering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scattering) of waves from a large crystal lattice. It describes how the superposition of wave fronts scattered by lattice planes leads to a strict relation between the wavelength and scattering angle. This law was initially formulated for X-rays, but it also applies to all types of [matter waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter_wave) including neutron and electron waves if there are a large number of atoms, as well as to visible light with artificial periodic microscale lattices.

## History

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bragg%27s_law&action=edit&section=1)] [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ee/Diffusion_rayleigh_et_diffraction.svg/500px-Diffusion_rayleigh_et_diffraction.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Diffusion_rayleigh_et_diffraction.svg)X-rays interact with the atoms in a [crystal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal).See also: [X-ray crystallography § History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_crystallography#History)

Bragg diffraction (also referred to as the **Bragg formulation of X-ray diffraction**) was first proposed by [Lawrence Bragg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Bragg) and his father, [William Henry Bragg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Henry_Bragg), in 1913[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-1) after their discovery that [crystalline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal) solids produced surprising patterns of reflected [X-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-rays) (in contrast to those produced with, for instance, a liquid). They found that these crystals, at certain specific wavelengths and incident angles, produced intense peaks of reflected radiation.

 [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/03/Braggs_Law2.svg/500px-Braggs_Law2.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Braggs_Law2.svg)According to the 2*θ* deviation, the phase shift causes constructive (left figure) or destructive (right figure) interferences.

Lawrence Bragg explained this result by modeling the crystal as a set of discrete parallel planes separated by a constant parameter d. He proposed that the incident X-ray radiation would produce a Bragg peak if reflections off the various planes interfered constructively. The interference is constructive when the phase difference between the wave reflected off different atomic planes is a multiple of 2*π*; this condition (see *Bragg condition* section below) was first presented by Lawrence Bragg on 11 November 1912 to the [Cambridge Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Philosophical_Society).[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-2) Due to its simplicity, Bragg's law provided a powerful new tool for determining [crystal lattices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_lattice) from X-ray diffraction data. Lawrence Bragg and his father, William Henry Bragg, were awarded the [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize) in physics in 1915 for their work in solving crystal structures beginning with [NaCl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_chloride), [ZnS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zinc_sulfide), and [diamond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond).[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-3) They are the only father-son team to jointly win.

The concept of Bragg diffraction applies equally to [neutron diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_diffraction)[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-4) and approximately to [electron diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_diffraction).[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-:0-5) In both cases the wavelengths are comparable with inter-atomic distances (~ 150 pm). Many other types of [matter waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter_wave) have also been shown to diffract,[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-6)[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-7) and also light from objects with a larger ordered structure such as [opals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opal).[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-Pieranski_1983-8)

## Bragg condition

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bragg%27s_law&action=edit&section=2)] [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c0/Bragg_diffraction_2.svg/500px-Bragg_diffraction_2.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bragg_diffraction_2.svg)Bragg diffraction[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-bragg-9): 16  Two beams with identical wavelength and phase approach a crystalline solid and are scattered off two different atoms within it. The lower beam traverses an extra length of 2*d*sin*θ*. Constructive interference occurs when this length is equal to an integer multiple of the wavelength of the radiation.

Bragg diffraction occurs when radiation of a [wavelength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelength) λ comparable to atomic spacings is scattered in a [specular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specular_reflection) fashion (mirror-like reflection) by planes of atoms in a crystalline material, and undergoes constructive interference.[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-moseley1913a-10) When the scattered waves are incident at a specific angle, they remain in phase and constructively [interfere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interference_(wave_propagation)). The [glancing angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glancing_angle_(optics)) θ (see figure on the right, and note that this differs from the convention in [Snell's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snell's_law) where θ is measured from the surface normal), the wavelength λ, and the "grating constant" d of the crystal are connected by the relation:[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-Mose1913-11): 1026     n λ = 2 d sin ⁡ θ   {\displaystyle n\lambda =2d\sin \theta }  where     n   {\displaystyle n}   is the [diffraction order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction_order) (    n = 1   {\displaystyle n=1}   is first order,     n = 2   {\displaystyle n=2}   is second order,[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-moseley1913a-10): 221      n = 3   {\displaystyle n=3}   is third order[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-Mose1913-11): 1028 ). This equation, Bragg's law, describes the condition on *θ* for constructive interference.[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-12)

A map of the intensities of the scattered waves as a function of their angle is called a diffraction pattern. Strong intensities known as Bragg peaks are obtained in the diffraction pattern when the scattering angles satisfy Bragg condition. This is a special case of the more general [Laue equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laue_equations), and the Laue equations can be shown to reduce to the Bragg condition with additional assumptions.[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-:1-13)

## Derivation

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bragg%27s_law&action=edit&section=3)]

In Bragg's original paper he describes his approach as a [Huygens's construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huygens's_construction) for a reflected wave.[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-Bragg-1914-14): 46  Suppose that a [plane wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_wave) (of any type) is incident on planes of [lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_lattice) points, with separation     d   {\displaystyle d}  , at an angle     θ   {\displaystyle \theta }   as shown in the Figure. Points **A** and **C** are on one plane, and **B** is on the plane below. Points **ABCC'** form a [quadrilateral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrilateral).[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-15): 69

 [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0a/Bragg%27s_law.svg/960px-Bragg%27s_law.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bragg's_law.svg)

There will be a path difference between the [ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_(optics)) that gets reflected along **AC'** and the ray that gets transmitted along **AB**, then reflected along **BC**. This path difference is     ( A B + B C ) −  (  A  C ′   )   .   {\displaystyle (AB+BC)-\left(AC'\right)\,.}

The two separate waves will arrive at a point (infinitely far from these lattice planes) with the same [phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_(waves)), and hence undergo [constructive interference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructive_interference), if and only if this path difference is equal to any integer value of the [wavelength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelength), i.e.     n λ = ( A B + B C ) −  (  A  C ′   )    {\displaystyle n\lambda =(AB+BC)-\left(AC'\right)}

where     n   {\displaystyle n}   and     λ   {\displaystyle \lambda }   are an integer and the wavelength of the incident wave respectively.

Therefore, from the geometry     A B = B C =   d  sin ⁡ θ      and   A C =    2 d   tan ⁡ θ     ,   {\displaystyle AB=BC={\frac {d}{\sin \theta }}{\text{ and }}AC={\frac {2d}{\tan \theta }}\,,}

from which it follows that     A  C ′  = A C ⋅ cos ⁡ θ =    2 d   tan ⁡ θ    cos ⁡ θ =  (     2 d   sin ⁡ θ    cos ⁡ θ  )  cos ⁡ θ =    2 d   sin ⁡ θ     cos  2   ⁡ θ  .   {\displaystyle AC'=AC\cdot \cos \theta ={\frac {2d}{\tan \theta }}\cos \theta =\left({\frac {2d}{\sin \theta }}\cos \theta \right)\cos \theta ={\frac {2d}{\sin \theta }}\cos ^{2}\theta \,.}

Putting everything together,     n λ =    2 d   sin ⁡ θ    −    2 d   sin ⁡ θ     cos  2   ⁡ θ =    2 d   sin ⁡ θ     (  1 −  cos  2   ⁡ θ  )  =    2 d   sin ⁡ θ     sin  2   ⁡ θ   {\displaystyle n\lambda ={\frac {2d}{\sin \theta }}-{\frac {2d}{\sin \theta }}\cos ^{2}\theta ={\frac {2d}{\sin \theta }}\left(1-\cos ^{2}\theta \right)={\frac {2d}{\sin \theta }}\sin ^{2}\theta }

which simplifies to     n λ = 2 d sin ⁡ θ  ,   {\displaystyle n\lambda =2d\sin \theta \,,}   which is Bragg's law shown above.

If only two planes of atoms were diffracting, as shown in the Figure then the transition from constructive to destructive interference would be gradual as a function of angle, with gentle [maxima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxima_and_minima) at the Bragg angles. However, since many atomic planes are participating in most real materials, sharp peaks are typical.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-:0-5)[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-:1-13)

A rigorous derivation from the more general Laue equations is available (see page: [Laue equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laue_equations)).

## Beyond Bragg's law

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bragg%27s_law&action=edit&section=4)] See also: [Electron diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_diffraction)[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/32/DifraccionElectronesMET.jpg/250px-DifraccionElectronesMET.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:DifraccionElectronesMET.jpg)Typical selected area electron diffraction pattern. Each spot corresponds to a different diffracted direction.

The Bragg condition is correct for very large crystals. Because the scattering of X-rays and neutrons is relatively weak, in many cases quite large crystals with sizes of 100 nm or more are used. While there can be additional effects due to [crystal defects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_defect), these are often quite small. In contrast, electrons interact thousands of times more strongly with solids than X-rays,[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-:0-5) and also lose energy ([inelastic scattering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_energy_loss_spectroscopy)).[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-16) Therefore, samples used in [transmission electron diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_diffraction) are much thinner. Typical diffraction patterns, for instance the Figure, show spots for different directions ([plane waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_wave)) of the electrons leaving a crystal. The angles that Bragg's law predicts are still approximately right, but in general there is a lattice of spots which are close to projections of the [reciprocal lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocal_lattice) that is at right angles to the direction of the electron beam. (In contrast, Bragg's law predicts that only one or perhaps two would be present, not simultaneously tens to hundreds.) With [low-energy electron diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-energy_electron_diffraction) where the electron energies are typically 30-1000 [electron volts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronvolt), the result is similar with the electrons reflected back from a surface.[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-17) Also similar is [reflection high-energy electron diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_high-energy_electron_diffraction) which typically leads to rings of diffraction spots.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-18)

With X-rays the effect of having small crystals is described by the [Scherrer equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scherrer_equation).[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-:1-13)[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-19)[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-20) This leads to broadening of the Bragg peaks which can be used to estimate the size of the crystals.

## Bragg scattering of visible light by colloids

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bragg%27s_law&action=edit&section=5)]

A [colloidal crystal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colloidal_crystal) is a highly [ordered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_(crystal_lattice)) array of particles that forms over a long range (from a few [millimeters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millimeters) to one [centimeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centimeter) in length); colloidal crystals have appearance and properties roughly [analogous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analogous) to their atomic or molecular counterparts.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-Pieranski_1983-8) It has been known for many years that, due to [repulsive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb's_law) [Coulombic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulombic) interactions, [electrically charged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrically_charged) [macromolecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macromolecules) in an [aqueous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqueous) environment can exhibit long-range [crystal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal)-like correlations, with interparticle separation distances often being considerably greater than the individual particle diameter. Periodic arrays of spherical particles give rise to [interstitial voids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacancy_defect) (the spaces between the particles), which act as a natural [diffraction grating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction_grating) for [visible light waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_spectrum), when the interstitial spacing is of the same [order of magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_magnitude) as the [incident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_of_incidence_(optics)) lightwave.[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-Hiltner_1969-21)[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-Aksay_1984-22)[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-LuckKlier1963-23) In these cases brilliant [iridescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iridescence) (or play of colours) is attributed to the diffraction and [constructive interference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructive_interference) of visible lightwaves according to Bragg's law, in a matter analogous to the [scattering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scattering) of [X-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-rays) in crystalline solid. The effects occur at visible wavelengths because the interplanar spacing d is much larger than for true crystals. Precious [opal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opal) is one example of a colloidal crystal with optical effects.

## Volume Bragg gratings

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bragg%27s_law&action=edit&section=6)] Main article: [Volume hologram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume_hologram)

Volume Bragg gratings (VBG) or [volume holographic gratings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume_hologram) (VHG) consist of a volume where there is a periodic change in the [refractive index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refractive_index). Depending on the orientation of the refractive index modulation, VBG can be used either to [transmit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmission_coefficient) or [reflect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_(physics)) a small bandwidth of [wavelengths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelengths).[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-24) Bragg's law (adapted for volume hologram) dictates which wavelength will be diffracted:[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-:2-25)

2 Λ sin ⁡ ( θ + φ ) = m  λ  B    ,   {\displaystyle 2\Lambda \sin(\theta +\varphi )=m\lambda _{B}\,,}

where m is the Bragg order (a positive integer), *λ*B the diffracted [wavelength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelength), Λ the fringe spacing of the grating, θ the angle between the incident beam and the normal (**N**) of the entrance surface and φ the angle between the normal and the grating vector (**KG**). Radiation that does not match Bragg's law will pass through the VBG undiffracted. The output wavelength can be tuned over a few hundred nanometers by changing the incident angle (θ). VBG are being used to produce [widely tunable laser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunable_laser#Widely_tunable_lasers) source or perform global [hyperspectral imagery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperspectral_imaging) (see [Photon etc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon_etc.)).[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-:2-25)

## Selection rules and practical crystallography

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bragg%27s_law&action=edit&section=7)] Main article: [X-ray crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_crystallography)

The measurement of the angles can be used to determine crystal structure, see [x-ray crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_crystallography) for more details.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-:0-5)[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-:1-13) As a simple example, Bragg's law, as stated above, can be used to obtain the lattice spacing of a particular [cubic system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_system) through the following relation:

d =   a   h  2   +  k  2   +  ℓ  2       ,   {\displaystyle d={\frac {a}{\sqrt {h^{2}+k^{2}+\ell ^{2}}}}\,,}

where     a   {\displaystyle a}   is the lattice spacing of the [cubic crystal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_crystal), and h, k, and ℓ are the [Miller indices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miller_indices) of the Bragg plane. Combining this relation with Bragg's law gives:

(   λ  2 a    )   2   =   (   λ  2 d    )   2     1   h  2   +  k  2   +  ℓ  2        {\displaystyle \left({\frac {\lambda }{2a}}\right)^{2}=\left({\frac {\lambda }{2d}}\right)^{2}{\frac {1}{h^{2}+k^{2}+\ell ^{2}}}}

One can derive selection rules for the [Miller indices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miller_indices) for different cubic [Bravais lattices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bravais_lattices) as well as many others, a few of the selection rules are given in the table below.

  Selection rules for the Miller indices Bravais lattices Example compounds Allowed reflections Forbidden reflections  Simple cubic Po Any *h*, *k*, *ℓ* None  Body-centered cubic Fe, W, Ta, Cr *h* + *k* + *ℓ* = even *h* + *k* + *ℓ* = odd  Face-centered cubic (FCC) Cu, Al, Ni, NaCl, LiH, PbS *h*, *k*, *ℓ* all odd or all even *h*, *k*, *ℓ* mixed odd and even  Diamond FCC Si, Ge All odd, or all even with *h* + *k* + *ℓ* = 4*n* *h*, *k*, *ℓ* mixed odd and even, or all even with *h* + *k* + *ℓ* ≠ 4*n*  [Hexagonal lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexagonal_lattice) Ti, Zr, Cd, Be *ℓ* even, *h* + 2*k* ≠ 3*n* *h* + 2*k* = 3*n* for odd *ℓ*

These selection rules can be used for any crystal with the given crystal structure. KCl has a face-centered cubic [Bravais lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bravais_lattice). However, the K+ and the Cl− ion have the same number of electrons and are quite close in size, so that the diffraction pattern becomes essentially the same as for a simple cubic structure with half the lattice parameter. Selection rules for other structures can be referenced elsewhere, or [derived](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structure_factor). Lattice spacing for the other [crystal systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_system) can be found [here](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_structure#Interplanar_spacing).

In many areas of science, **Bragg's law** – also known as **[Wulff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Wulff)–Bragg's condition** or **[Laue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_von_Laue)–Bragg interference** – is a special case of [Laue diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laue_diffraction) that gives the angles for coherent [scattering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scattering) of waves from a large crystal lattice. It describes how the superposition of wave fronts scattered by lattice planes leads to a strict relation between the wavelength and scattering angle. This law was initially formulated for X-rays, but it also applies to all types of [matter waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter_wave) including neutron and electron waves if there are a large number of atoms, as well as to visible light with artificial periodic microscale lattices.

History

# History

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bragg%27s_law&action=edit&section=1)] [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ee/Diffusion_rayleigh_et_diffraction.svg/500px-Diffusion_rayleigh_et_diffraction.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Diffusion_rayleigh_et_diffraction.svg)X-rays interact with the atoms in a [crystal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal).See also: [X-ray crystallography § History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_crystallography#History)

Bragg diffraction (also referred to as the **Bragg formulation of X-ray diffraction**) was first proposed by [Lawrence Bragg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Bragg) and his father, [William Henry Bragg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Henry_Bragg), in 1913[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-1) after their discovery that [crystalline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal) solids produced surprising patterns of reflected [X-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-rays) (in contrast to those produced with, for instance, a liquid). They found that these crystals, at certain specific wavelengths and incident angles, produced intense peaks of reflected radiation.

 [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/03/Braggs_Law2.svg/500px-Braggs_Law2.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Braggs_Law2.svg)According to the 2*θ* deviation, the phase shift causes constructive (left figure) or destructive (right figure) interferences.

Lawrence Bragg explained this result by modeling the crystal as a set of discrete parallel planes separated by a constant parameter d. He proposed that the incident X-ray radiation would produce a Bragg peak if reflections off the various planes interfered constructively. The interference is constructive when the phase difference between the wave reflected off different atomic planes is a multiple of 2*π*; this condition (see *Bragg condition* section below) was first presented by Lawrence Bragg on 11 November 1912 to the [Cambridge Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Philosophical_Society).[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-2) Due to its simplicity, Bragg's law provided a powerful new tool for determining [crystal lattices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_lattice) from X-ray diffraction data. Lawrence Bragg and his father, William Henry Bragg, were awarded the [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize) in physics in 1915 for their work in solving crystal structures beginning with [NaCl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_chloride), [ZnS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zinc_sulfide), and [diamond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond).[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-3) They are the only father-son team to jointly win.

The concept of Bragg diffraction applies equally to [neutron diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_diffraction)[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-4) and approximately to [electron diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_diffraction).[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-:0-5) In both cases the wavelengths are comparable with inter-atomic distances (~ 150 pm). Many other types of [matter waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter_wave) have also been shown to diffract,[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-6)[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-7) and also light from objects with a larger ordered structure such as [opals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opal).[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-Pieranski_1983-8)

Bragg condition

# Bragg condition

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bragg%27s_law&action=edit&section=2)] [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c0/Bragg_diffraction_2.svg/500px-Bragg_diffraction_2.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bragg_diffraction_2.svg)Bragg diffraction[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-bragg-9): 16  Two beams with identical wavelength and phase approach a crystalline solid and are scattered off two different atoms within it. The lower beam traverses an extra length of 2*d*sin*θ*. Constructive interference occurs when this length is equal to an integer multiple of the wavelength of the radiation.

Bragg diffraction occurs when radiation of a [wavelength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelength) λ comparable to atomic spacings is scattered in a [specular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specular_reflection) fashion (mirror-like reflection) by planes of atoms in a crystalline material, and undergoes constructive interference.[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-moseley1913a-10) When the scattered waves are incident at a specific angle, they remain in phase and constructively [interfere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interference_(wave_propagation)). The [glancing angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glancing_angle_(optics)) θ (see figure on the right, and note that this differs from the convention in [Snell's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snell's_law) where θ is measured from the surface normal), the wavelength λ, and the "grating constant" d of the crystal are connected by the relation:[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-Mose1913-11): 1026     n λ = 2 d sin ⁡ θ   {\displaystyle n\lambda =2d\sin \theta }  where     n   {\displaystyle n}   is the [diffraction order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction_order) (    n = 1   {\displaystyle n=1}   is first order,     n = 2   {\displaystyle n=2}   is second order,[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-moseley1913a-10): 221      n = 3   {\displaystyle n=3}   is third order[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-Mose1913-11): 1028 ). This equation, Bragg's law, describes the condition on *θ* for constructive interference.[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-12)

A map of the intensities of the scattered waves as a function of their angle is called a diffraction pattern. Strong intensities known as Bragg peaks are obtained in the diffraction pattern when the scattering angles satisfy Bragg condition. This is a special case of the more general [Laue equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laue_equations), and the Laue equations can be shown to reduce to the Bragg condition with additional assumptions.[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-:1-13)

Derivation

# Derivation

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bragg%27s_law&action=edit&section=3)]

In Bragg's original paper he describes his approach as a [Huygens's construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huygens's_construction) for a reflected wave.[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-Bragg-1914-14): 46  Suppose that a [plane wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_wave) (of any type) is incident on planes of [lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_lattice) points, with separation     d   {\displaystyle d}  , at an angle     θ   {\displaystyle \theta }   as shown in the Figure. Points **A** and **C** are on one plane, and **B** is on the plane below. Points **ABCC'** form a [quadrilateral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrilateral).[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-15): 69

 [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0a/Bragg%27s_law.svg/960px-Bragg%27s_law.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bragg's_law.svg)

There will be a path difference between the [ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_(optics)) that gets reflected along **AC'** and the ray that gets transmitted along **AB**, then reflected along **BC**. This path difference is     ( A B + B C ) −  (  A  C ′   )   .   {\displaystyle (AB+BC)-\left(AC'\right)\,.}

The two separate waves will arrive at a point (infinitely far from these lattice planes) with the same [phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_(waves)), and hence undergo [constructive interference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructive_interference), if and only if this path difference is equal to any integer value of the [wavelength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelength), i.e.     n λ = ( A B + B C ) −  (  A  C ′   )    {\displaystyle n\lambda =(AB+BC)-\left(AC'\right)}

where     n   {\displaystyle n}   and     λ   {\displaystyle \lambda }   are an integer and the wavelength of the incident wave respectively.

Therefore, from the geometry     A B = B C =   d  sin ⁡ θ      and   A C =    2 d   tan ⁡ θ     ,   {\displaystyle AB=BC={\frac {d}{\sin \theta }}{\text{ and }}AC={\frac {2d}{\tan \theta }}\,,}

from which it follows that     A  C ′  = A C ⋅ cos ⁡ θ =    2 d   tan ⁡ θ    cos ⁡ θ =  (     2 d   sin ⁡ θ    cos ⁡ θ  )  cos ⁡ θ =    2 d   sin ⁡ θ     cos  2   ⁡ θ  .   {\displaystyle AC'=AC\cdot \cos \theta ={\frac {2d}{\tan \theta }}\cos \theta =\left({\frac {2d}{\sin \theta }}\cos \theta \right)\cos \theta ={\frac {2d}{\sin \theta }}\cos ^{2}\theta \,.}

Putting everything together,     n λ =    2 d   sin ⁡ θ    −    2 d   sin ⁡ θ     cos  2   ⁡ θ =    2 d   sin ⁡ θ     (  1 −  cos  2   ⁡ θ  )  =    2 d   sin ⁡ θ     sin  2   ⁡ θ   {\displaystyle n\lambda ={\frac {2d}{\sin \theta }}-{\frac {2d}{\sin \theta }}\cos ^{2}\theta ={\frac {2d}{\sin \theta }}\left(1-\cos ^{2}\theta \right)={\frac {2d}{\sin \theta }}\sin ^{2}\theta }

which simplifies to     n λ = 2 d sin ⁡ θ  ,   {\displaystyle n\lambda =2d\sin \theta \,,}   which is Bragg's law shown above.

If only two planes of atoms were diffracting, as shown in the Figure then the transition from constructive to destructive interference would be gradual as a function of angle, with gentle [maxima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxima_and_minima) at the Bragg angles. However, since many atomic planes are participating in most real materials, sharp peaks are typical.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-:0-5)[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-:1-13)

A rigorous derivation from the more general Laue equations is available (see page: [Laue equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laue_equations)).

Beyond Bragg's law

# Beyond Bragg's law

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bragg%27s_law&action=edit&section=4)] See also: [Electron diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_diffraction)[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/32/DifraccionElectronesMET.jpg/250px-DifraccionElectronesMET.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:DifraccionElectronesMET.jpg)Typical selected area electron diffraction pattern. Each spot corresponds to a different diffracted direction.

The Bragg condition is correct for very large crystals. Because the scattering of X-rays and neutrons is relatively weak, in many cases quite large crystals with sizes of 100 nm or more are used. While there can be additional effects due to [crystal defects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_defect), these are often quite small. In contrast, electrons interact thousands of times more strongly with solids than X-rays,[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-:0-5) and also lose energy ([inelastic scattering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_energy_loss_spectroscopy)).[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-16) Therefore, samples used in [transmission electron diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_diffraction) are much thinner. Typical diffraction patterns, for instance the Figure, show spots for different directions ([plane waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_wave)) of the electrons leaving a crystal. The angles that Bragg's law predicts are still approximately right, but in general there is a lattice of spots which are close to projections of the [reciprocal lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocal_lattice) that is at right angles to the direction of the electron beam. (In contrast, Bragg's law predicts that only one or perhaps two would be present, not simultaneously tens to hundreds.) With [low-energy electron diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-energy_electron_diffraction) where the electron energies are typically 30-1000 [electron volts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronvolt), the result is similar with the electrons reflected back from a surface.[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-17) Also similar is [reflection high-energy electron diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_high-energy_electron_diffraction) which typically leads to rings of diffraction spots.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-18)

With X-rays the effect of having small crystals is described by the [Scherrer equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scherrer_equation).[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-:1-13)[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-19)[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-20) This leads to broadening of the Bragg peaks which can be used to estimate the size of the crystals.

Bragg scattering of visible light by colloids

# Bragg scattering of visible light by colloids

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bragg%27s_law&action=edit&section=5)]

A [colloidal crystal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colloidal_crystal) is a highly [ordered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_(crystal_lattice)) array of particles that forms over a long range (from a few [millimeters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millimeters) to one [centimeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centimeter) in length); colloidal crystals have appearance and properties roughly [analogous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analogous) to their atomic or molecular counterparts.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-Pieranski_1983-8) It has been known for many years that, due to [repulsive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb's_law) [Coulombic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulombic) interactions, [electrically charged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrically_charged) [macromolecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macromolecules) in an [aqueous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqueous) environment can exhibit long-range [crystal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal)-like correlations, with interparticle separation distances often being considerably greater than the individual particle diameter. Periodic arrays of spherical particles give rise to [interstitial voids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacancy_defect) (the spaces between the particles), which act as a natural [diffraction grating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction_grating) for [visible light waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_spectrum), when the interstitial spacing is of the same [order of magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_magnitude) as the [incident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_of_incidence_(optics)) lightwave.[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-Hiltner_1969-21)[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-Aksay_1984-22)[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-LuckKlier1963-23) In these cases brilliant [iridescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iridescence) (or play of colours) is attributed to the diffraction and [constructive interference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructive_interference) of visible lightwaves according to Bragg's law, in a matter analogous to the [scattering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scattering) of [X-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-rays) in crystalline solid. The effects occur at visible wavelengths because the interplanar spacing d is much larger than for true crystals. Precious [opal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opal) is one example of a colloidal crystal with optical effects.

Volume Bragg gratings

# Volume Bragg gratings

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bragg%27s_law&action=edit&section=6)] Main article: [Volume hologram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume_hologram)

Volume Bragg gratings (VBG) or [volume holographic gratings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume_hologram) (VHG) consist of a volume where there is a periodic change in the [refractive index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refractive_index). Depending on the orientation of the refractive index modulation, VBG can be used either to [transmit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmission_coefficient) or [reflect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_(physics)) a small bandwidth of [wavelengths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelengths).[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-24) Bragg's law (adapted for volume hologram) dictates which wavelength will be diffracted:[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-:2-25)

2 Λ sin ⁡ ( θ + φ ) = m  λ  B    ,   {\displaystyle 2\Lambda \sin(\theta +\varphi )=m\lambda _{B}\,,}

where m is the Bragg order (a positive integer), *λ*B the diffracted [wavelength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelength), Λ the fringe spacing of the grating, θ the angle between the incident beam and the normal (**N**) of the entrance surface and φ the angle between the normal and the grating vector (**KG**). Radiation that does not match Bragg's law will pass through the VBG undiffracted. The output wavelength can be tuned over a few hundred nanometers by changing the incident angle (θ). VBG are being used to produce [widely tunable laser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunable_laser#Widely_tunable_lasers) source or perform global [hyperspectral imagery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperspectral_imaging) (see [Photon etc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon_etc.)).[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-:2-25)

Selection rules and practical crystallography

# Selection rules and practical crystallography

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bragg%27s_law&action=edit&section=7)] Main article: [X-ray crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_crystallography)

The measurement of the angles can be used to determine crystal structure, see [x-ray crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_crystallography) for more details.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-:0-5)[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg%27s_law#cite_note-:1-13) As a simple example, Bragg's law, as stated above, can be used to obtain the lattice spacing of a particular [cubic system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_system) through the following relation:

d =   a   h  2   +  k  2   +  ℓ  2       ,   {\displaystyle d={\frac {a}{\sqrt {h^{2}+k^{2}+\ell ^{2}}}}\,,}

where     a   {\displaystyle a}   is the lattice spacing of the [cubic crystal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_crystal), and h, k, and ℓ are the [Miller indices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miller_indices) of the Bragg plane. Combining this relation with Bragg's law gives:

(   λ  2 a    )   2   =   (   λ  2 d    )   2     1   h  2   +  k  2   +  ℓ  2        {\displaystyle \left({\frac {\lambda }{2a}}\right)^{2}=\left({\frac {\lambda }{2d}}\right)^{2}{\frac {1}{h^{2}+k^{2}+\ell ^{2}}}}

One can derive selection rules for the [Miller indices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miller_indices) for different cubic [Bravais lattices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bravais_lattices) as well as many others, a few of the selection rules are given in the table below.

  Selection rules for the Miller indices Bravais lattices Example compounds Allowed reflections Forbidden reflections  Simple cubic Po Any *h*, *k*, *ℓ* None  Body-centered cubic Fe, W, Ta, Cr *h* + *k* + *ℓ* = even *h* + *k* + *ℓ* = odd  Face-centered cubic (FCC) Cu, Al, Ni, NaCl, LiH, PbS *h*, *k*, *ℓ* all odd or all even *h*, *k*, *ℓ* mixed odd and even  Diamond FCC Si, Ge All odd, or all even with *h* + *k* + *ℓ* = 4*n* *h*, *k*, *ℓ* mixed odd and even, or all even with *h* + *k* + *ℓ* ≠ 4*n*  [Hexagonal lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexagonal_lattice) Ti, Zr, Cd, Be *ℓ* even, *h* + 2*k* ≠ 3*n* *h* + 2*k* = 3*n* for odd *ℓ*

These selection rules can be used for any crystal with the given crystal structure. KCl has a face-centered cubic [Bravais lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bravais_lattice). However, the K+ and the Cl− ion have the same number of electrons and are quite close in size, so that the diffraction pattern becomes essentially the same as for a simple cubic structure with half the lattice parameter. Selection rules for other structures can be referenced elsewhere, or [derived](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structure_factor). Lattice spacing for the other [crystal systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_system) can be found [here](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_structure#Interplanar_spacing).
