# Stable manifold

 [Edit links](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Special:EntityPage/Q3554830#sitelinks-wikipedia)                          From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia (Redirected from [Stable and unstable sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Stable_and_unstable_sets&redirect=no))  Formalization of the idea of an attractor or repellor in dynamical systems

In [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics), and in particular the study of [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems), the idea of *stable and unstable sets* or **stable and unstable manifolds** give a formal mathematical definition to the general notions embodied in the idea of an [attractor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attractor) or [repellor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repellor). In the case of [hyperbolic dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_dynamics), the corresponding notion is that of the [hyperbolic set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_set).

 [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/80/Hyperbolic_flow_example%2C_illustrating_stable_and_unstable_manifolds.png/250px-Hyperbolic_flow_example%2C_illustrating_stable_and_unstable_manifolds.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Hyperbolic_flow_example,_illustrating_stable_and_unstable_manifolds.png)Example hyperbolic flow, illustrating stable and unstable manifolds. The vector field equation is     ( x + exp ⁡ ( − y ) , − y )   {\displaystyle (x+\exp(-y),-y)}  . The unstable manifold is the x-axis, and the stable manifold is the other asymptotic curve crossing the x-axis at the point (-1, 0).

## Physical example

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Stable_manifold&action=edit&section=1)]

The gravitational [tidal forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_force) acting on the [rings of Saturn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rings_of_Saturn) provide an easy-to-visualize physical example. The tidal forces flatten the ring into the equatorial plane, even as they stretch it out in the radial direction. Imagining the rings to be sand or gravel particles ("dust") in orbit around Saturn, the tidal forces are such that any perturbations that push particles above or below the equatorial plane results in that particle feeling a restoring force, pushing it back into the plane. Particles effectively oscillate in a harmonic well, damped by collisions. The stable direction is perpendicular to the ring. The unstable direction is along any radius, where forces stretch and pull particles apart. Two particles that start very near each other in [phase space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_space) will experience radial forces causing them to diverge, radially. These forces have a positive [Lyapunov exponent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyapunov_exponent); the trajectories lie on a hyperbolic manifold, and the movement of particles is essentially [chaotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaos_theory), wandering through the rings. The [center manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_manifold) is tangential to the rings, with particles experiencing neither compression nor stretching. This allows second-order gravitational forces to dominate, and so particles can be entrained by moons or moonlets in the rings, [phase locking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_map) to them. The gravitational forces of the moons effectively provide a regularly repeating small kick, each time around the orbit, akin to a [kicked rotor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kicked_rotor), such as found in a [phase-locked loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase-locked_loop).

The discrete-time motion of particles in the ring can be approximated by the [Poincaré map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincar%C3%A9_map). The map effectively provides the [transfer matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfer_matrix) of the system. The eigenvector associated with the largest eigenvalue of the matrix is the [Frobenius–Perron eigenvector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frobenius-Perron_eigenvector), which is also the [invariant measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invariant_measure), *i.e* the actual density of the particles in the ring. All other eigenvectors of the transfer matrix have smaller eigenvalues, and correspond to decaying modes.

## Definition

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Stable_manifold&action=edit&section=2)]

The following provides a definition for the case of a system that is either an [iterated function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iterated_function) or has discrete-time dynamics. Similar notions apply for systems whose time evolution is given by a [flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_(mathematics)).

Let     X   {\displaystyle X}   be a [topological space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_space), and     f : X → X   {\displaystyle f\colon X\to X}   a [homeomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphism). If     p   {\displaystyle p}   is a [fixed point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_point_(mathematics)) for     f   {\displaystyle f}  , the **stable set of     p   {\displaystyle p}** is defined by

      W  s   ( f , p ) = { q ∈ X :  f  n   ( q ) → p    as    n → ∞ }   {\displaystyle W^{s}(f,p)=\{q\in X:f^{n}(q)\to p{\mbox{ as }}n\to \infty \}}

and the **unstable set of     p   {\displaystyle p}** is defined by

      W  u   ( f , p ) = { q ∈ X :  f  − n   ( q ) → p    as    n → ∞ } .   {\displaystyle W^{u}(f,p)=\{q\in X:f^{-n}(q)\to p{\mbox{ as }}n\to \infty \}.}

Here,      f  − 1     {\displaystyle f^{-1}}   denotes the [inverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_function) of the function     f   {\displaystyle f}  , i.e.     f ∘  f  − 1   =  f  − 1   ∘ f = i  d  X     {\displaystyle f\circ f^{-1}=f^{-1}\circ f=id_{X}}  , where     i  d  X     {\displaystyle id_{X}}   is the identity map on     X   {\displaystyle X}  .

If     p   {\displaystyle p}   is a [periodic point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_point) of least period     k   {\displaystyle k}  , then it is a fixed point of      f  k     {\displaystyle f^{k}}  , and the stable and unstable sets of     p   {\displaystyle p}   are defined by

      W  s   ( f , p ) =  W  s   (  f  k   , p )   {\displaystyle W^{s}(f,p)=W^{s}(f^{k},p)}

and

      W  u   ( f , p ) =  W  u   (  f  k   , p ) .   {\displaystyle W^{u}(f,p)=W^{u}(f^{k},p).}

Given a [neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_(mathematics))     U   {\displaystyle U}   of     p   {\displaystyle p}  , the **local stable and unstable sets** of     p   {\displaystyle p}   are defined by

      W   l o c    s   ( f , p , U ) = { q ∈ U :  f  n   ( q ) ∈ U    for each    n ≥ 0 }   {\displaystyle W_{\mathrm {loc} }^{s}(f,p,U)=\{q\in U:f^{n}(q)\in U{\mbox{ for each }}n\geq 0\}}

and

      W   l o c    u   ( f , p , U ) =  W   l o c    s   (  f  − 1   , p , U ) .   {\displaystyle W_{\mathrm {loc} }^{u}(f,p,U)=W_{\mathrm {loc} }^{s}(f^{-1},p,U).}

If     X   {\displaystyle X}   is [metrizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrizable), we can define the stable and unstable sets for any point by

      W  s   ( f , p ) = { q ∈ X : d (  f  n   ( q ) ,  f  n   ( p ) ) → 0    for    n → ∞ }   {\displaystyle W^{s}(f,p)=\{q\in X:d(f^{n}(q),f^{n}(p))\to 0{\mbox{ for }}n\to \infty \}}

and

      W  u   ( f , p ) =  W  s   (  f  − 1   , p ) ,   {\displaystyle W^{u}(f,p)=W^{s}(f^{-1},p),}

where     d   {\displaystyle d}   is a [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_(mathematics)) for     X   {\displaystyle X}  . This definition clearly coincides with the previous one when     p   {\displaystyle p}   is a periodic point.

Suppose now that     X   {\displaystyle X}   is a [compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_space) [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold), and     f   {\displaystyle f}   is a        C    k     {\displaystyle {\mathcal {C}}^{k}}   [diffeomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphism),     k ≥ 1   {\displaystyle k\geq 1}  . If     p   {\displaystyle p}   is a hyperbolic periodic point, the [stable manifold theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stable_manifold_theorem) assures that for some neighborhood     U   {\displaystyle U}   of     p   {\displaystyle p}  , the local stable and unstable sets are        C    k     {\displaystyle {\mathcal {C}}^{k}}   embedded disks, whose [tangent spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_space) at     p   {\displaystyle p}   are      E  s     {\displaystyle E^{s}}   and      E  u     {\displaystyle E^{u}}   (the stable and unstable spaces of     D f ( p )   {\displaystyle Df(p)}  ), respectively; moreover, they vary continuously (in a certain sense) in a neighborhood of     f   {\displaystyle f}   in the        C    k     {\displaystyle {\mathcal {C}}^{k}}   topology of       D i f f   k   ( X )   {\displaystyle \mathrm {Diff} ^{k}(X)}   (the space of all        C    k     {\displaystyle {\mathcal {C}}^{k}}   diffeomorphisms from     X   {\displaystyle X}   to itself). Finally, the stable and unstable sets are        C    k     {\displaystyle {\mathcal {C}}^{k}}   injectively immersed disks. This is why they are commonly called **stable and unstable manifolds**. This result is also valid for nonperiodic points, as long as they lie in some [hyperbolic set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_set) (stable manifold theorem for hyperbolic sets).

## Remark

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Stable_manifold&action=edit&section=3)]

If     X   {\displaystyle X}   is a (finite-dimensional) [vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_space) and     f   {\displaystyle f}   an isomorphism, its stable and unstable sets are called stable space and unstable space, respectively.

In [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics), and in particular the study of [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems), the idea of *stable and unstable sets* or **stable and unstable manifolds** give a formal mathematical definition to the general notions embodied in the idea of an [attractor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attractor) or [repellor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repellor). In the case of [hyperbolic dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_dynamics), the corresponding notion is that of the [hyperbolic set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_set).

 [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/80/Hyperbolic_flow_example%2C_illustrating_stable_and_unstable_manifolds.png/250px-Hyperbolic_flow_example%2C_illustrating_stable_and_unstable_manifolds.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Hyperbolic_flow_example,_illustrating_stable_and_unstable_manifolds.png)Example hyperbolic flow, illustrating stable and unstable manifolds. The vector field equation is     ( x + exp ⁡ ( − y ) , − y )   {\displaystyle (x+\exp(-y),-y)}  . The unstable manifold is the x-axis, and the stable manifold is the other asymptotic curve crossing the x-axis at the point (-1, 0).

Physical example

# Physical example

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Stable_manifold&action=edit&section=1)]

The gravitational [tidal forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_force) acting on the [rings of Saturn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rings_of_Saturn) provide an easy-to-visualize physical example. The tidal forces flatten the ring into the equatorial plane, even as they stretch it out in the radial direction. Imagining the rings to be sand or gravel particles ("dust") in orbit around Saturn, the tidal forces are such that any perturbations that push particles above or below the equatorial plane results in that particle feeling a restoring force, pushing it back into the plane. Particles effectively oscillate in a harmonic well, damped by collisions. The stable direction is perpendicular to the ring. The unstable direction is along any radius, where forces stretch and pull particles apart. Two particles that start very near each other in [phase space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_space) will experience radial forces causing them to diverge, radially. These forces have a positive [Lyapunov exponent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyapunov_exponent); the trajectories lie on a hyperbolic manifold, and the movement of particles is essentially [chaotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaos_theory), wandering through the rings. The [center manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_manifold) is tangential to the rings, with particles experiencing neither compression nor stretching. This allows second-order gravitational forces to dominate, and so particles can be entrained by moons or moonlets in the rings, [phase locking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_map) to them. The gravitational forces of the moons effectively provide a regularly repeating small kick, each time around the orbit, akin to a [kicked rotor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kicked_rotor), such as found in a [phase-locked loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase-locked_loop).

The discrete-time motion of particles in the ring can be approximated by the [Poincaré map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincar%C3%A9_map). The map effectively provides the [transfer matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfer_matrix) of the system. The eigenvector associated with the largest eigenvalue of the matrix is the [Frobenius–Perron eigenvector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frobenius-Perron_eigenvector), which is also the [invariant measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invariant_measure), *i.e* the actual density of the particles in the ring. All other eigenvectors of the transfer matrix have smaller eigenvalues, and correspond to decaying modes.

Definition

# Definition

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Stable_manifold&action=edit&section=2)]

The following provides a definition for the case of a system that is either an [iterated function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iterated_function) or has discrete-time dynamics. Similar notions apply for systems whose time evolution is given by a [flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_(mathematics)).

Let     X   {\displaystyle X}   be a [topological space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_space), and     f : X → X   {\displaystyle f\colon X\to X}   a [homeomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphism). If     p   {\displaystyle p}   is a [fixed point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_point_(mathematics)) for     f   {\displaystyle f}  , the **stable set of     p   {\displaystyle p}** is defined by

      W  s   ( f , p ) = { q ∈ X :  f  n   ( q ) → p    as    n → ∞ }   {\displaystyle W^{s}(f,p)=\{q\in X:f^{n}(q)\to p{\mbox{ as }}n\to \infty \}}

and the **unstable set of     p   {\displaystyle p}** is defined by

      W  u   ( f , p ) = { q ∈ X :  f  − n   ( q ) → p    as    n → ∞ } .   {\displaystyle W^{u}(f,p)=\{q\in X:f^{-n}(q)\to p{\mbox{ as }}n\to \infty \}.}

Here,      f  − 1     {\displaystyle f^{-1}}   denotes the [inverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_function) of the function     f   {\displaystyle f}  , i.e.     f ∘  f  − 1   =  f  − 1   ∘ f = i  d  X     {\displaystyle f\circ f^{-1}=f^{-1}\circ f=id_{X}}  , where     i  d  X     {\displaystyle id_{X}}   is the identity map on     X   {\displaystyle X}  .

If     p   {\displaystyle p}   is a [periodic point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_point) of least period     k   {\displaystyle k}  , then it is a fixed point of      f  k     {\displaystyle f^{k}}  , and the stable and unstable sets of     p   {\displaystyle p}   are defined by

      W  s   ( f , p ) =  W  s   (  f  k   , p )   {\displaystyle W^{s}(f,p)=W^{s}(f^{k},p)}

and

      W  u   ( f , p ) =  W  u   (  f  k   , p ) .   {\displaystyle W^{u}(f,p)=W^{u}(f^{k},p).}

Given a [neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_(mathematics))     U   {\displaystyle U}   of     p   {\displaystyle p}  , the **local stable and unstable sets** of     p   {\displaystyle p}   are defined by

      W   l o c    s   ( f , p , U ) = { q ∈ U :  f  n   ( q ) ∈ U    for each    n ≥ 0 }   {\displaystyle W_{\mathrm {loc} }^{s}(f,p,U)=\{q\in U:f^{n}(q)\in U{\mbox{ for each }}n\geq 0\}}

and

      W   l o c    u   ( f , p , U ) =  W   l o c    s   (  f  − 1   , p , U ) .   {\displaystyle W_{\mathrm {loc} }^{u}(f,p,U)=W_{\mathrm {loc} }^{s}(f^{-1},p,U).}

If     X   {\displaystyle X}   is [metrizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrizable), we can define the stable and unstable sets for any point by

      W  s   ( f , p ) = { q ∈ X : d (  f  n   ( q ) ,  f  n   ( p ) ) → 0    for    n → ∞ }   {\displaystyle W^{s}(f,p)=\{q\in X:d(f^{n}(q),f^{n}(p))\to 0{\mbox{ for }}n\to \infty \}}

and

      W  u   ( f , p ) =  W  s   (  f  − 1   , p ) ,   {\displaystyle W^{u}(f,p)=W^{s}(f^{-1},p),}

where     d   {\displaystyle d}   is a [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_(mathematics)) for     X   {\displaystyle X}  . This definition clearly coincides with the previous one when     p   {\displaystyle p}   is a periodic point.

Suppose now that     X   {\displaystyle X}   is a [compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_space) [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold), and     f   {\displaystyle f}   is a        C    k     {\displaystyle {\mathcal {C}}^{k}}   [diffeomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphism),     k ≥ 1   {\displaystyle k\geq 1}  . If     p   {\displaystyle p}   is a hyperbolic periodic point, the [stable manifold theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stable_manifold_theorem) assures that for some neighborhood     U   {\displaystyle U}   of     p   {\displaystyle p}  , the local stable and unstable sets are        C    k     {\displaystyle {\mathcal {C}}^{k}}   embedded disks, whose [tangent spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_space) at     p   {\displaystyle p}   are      E  s     {\displaystyle E^{s}}   and      E  u     {\displaystyle E^{u}}   (the stable and unstable spaces of     D f ( p )   {\displaystyle Df(p)}  ), respectively; moreover, they vary continuously (in a certain sense) in a neighborhood of     f   {\displaystyle f}   in the        C    k     {\displaystyle {\mathcal {C}}^{k}}   topology of       D i f f   k   ( X )   {\displaystyle \mathrm {Diff} ^{k}(X)}   (the space of all        C    k     {\displaystyle {\mathcal {C}}^{k}}   diffeomorphisms from     X   {\displaystyle X}   to itself). Finally, the stable and unstable sets are        C    k     {\displaystyle {\mathcal {C}}^{k}}   injectively immersed disks. This is why they are commonly called **stable and unstable manifolds**. This result is also valid for nonperiodic points, as long as they lie in some [hyperbolic set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_set) (stable manifold theorem for hyperbolic sets).

Remark

# Remark

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Stable_manifold&action=edit&section=3)]

If     X   {\displaystyle X}   is a (finite-dimensional) [vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_space) and     f   {\displaystyle f}   an isomorphism, its stable and unstable sets are called stable space and unstable space, respectively.
